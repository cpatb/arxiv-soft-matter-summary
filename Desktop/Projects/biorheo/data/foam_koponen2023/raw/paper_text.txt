9 Abstract
10 Foam application of chemicals to the wet web is currently being developed for the paper and board
11 industry. An important part of this work is to understand the rheology of the used application foams.
12 Polyvinyl alcohol (PVOH) is widely used as a strength additive in paper and board, and it was the
13 main surfactant in this study. The PVOH foam density varied between 100 kg/m3 and 300 kg/m3 and
14 the dosage of PVOH varied between 0.5% to 6%. The foam viscosity and slip flow were determined
15 with a pipe rheometer using three pipe diameters. The slip velocity was quantified by recording the
16 foam motion in the vicinity of the wall of an acrylic pipe with a high-speed video camera. A
17 measurement setup was also built for measuring the slip flow indirectly in opaque pipes. General
18 formulas for the foam viscosity and slip flow, based on several physical quantities describing both
19 the foam and the base liquid, were obtained using dimensional analysis. Specifically, dimensionless
20 shear stress and dimensionless wall shear stress were found to be proportional to certain powers of
21 the capillary number and slip capillary number, respectively. The contribution of the slip flow to the
22 total flow rate was significant, especially with lower flow rates when most of the volumetric flow
23 was due to the slip. In the literature, many papers have suggested that there is no slip flow in steel
24 pipes. Our results suggest that this is due to the high pipe roughness used in those works. In our
25 measurements, the slip velocity of a smooth-walled steel pipe was equal to the slip in an acrylic pipe.
26 The obtained viscosity and slip models form a solid basis for developing and running various
27 industrial processes including foam application processes. For new foam recipes, quite a small
28 number of rheological measurements are needed to determine the model parameters.
29 1. Introduction
30 Liquid foams are two-phase systems composed of gas bubbles surrounded by a continuous liquid-
31 surfactant solution, where the role of the surfactant is to stabilize the foam by lowering the surface
32 tension between the gas and liquid phases (Weaire and Hutzler, 1999). When the (volumetric) gas
33 content  is below the jamming transition point  the gas bubbles are spherical and relatively free
J
34 to move. In this case, the foam is called bubbly liquid. For monodisperse bubbles  = 0.64 (Saint-
J
35 Jalmes and Durian, 1999), but in practice, the foams are usually polydisperse and the jamming
36 transition point is higher - slightly below 0.7 (Farr and Groot, 2009)(Lexis and Willenbacher, 2014).
37 The distinction between wet and dry foams is not uniquely defined but foams with  < 0.95 are
38 usually considered wet foams (Furuta et al., 2016). Above the jamming transition point, in wet foams,
39 the bubbles are still approximately spherical whereas in dry foams they are polyhedral.
40 Foams have several unique and useful properties (Schramm, 2005). The density of the foam can be
41 controlled by the air content - with high air content foams can be made very lightweight. Above the
42 jamming transition point, foams have yield stress, which immobilizes the foam under low-stress
[[PAGE 1]]
Chemical Engineering Science, autumn 2023, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ces.2023.119282
43 forces. Foams have also a high liquid-gas surface area, which can be utilized in transport processes.
44 The foams can also be stabilized by using polymers or small particles.
45 Foams have applications in many industrial systems. In many cases, the foam itself is the final
46 industrial product, such as a fire-fighting foam, a food foam, or a pharmacy/personal care product.
47 However, foams are also used as a temporary medium in many industrial processes – e.g., as a carrier
48 medium of chemicals and solids. In flotation processes, bubbly liquids are used for separating solids
49 from the fluid phase (Nguyen and Schulze, 2003). In the oil and gas industry, drilling foams are used
50 to remove and clean the drill cuttings from the downhole (Lyons et al., 2020). In the nonwoven and
51 paper and board industry the traditional carrier fluid, water, can be replaced with aqueous foams as
52 the carrier medium of fibers (Hjelt et al., 2021). This leads to improved sheet uniformity and decreases
53 the energy needed in the drying of the sheet due to the decreased usage of water. In the textile
54 industry, the foam-assisted application is used for spreading chemical solutions or particle dispersions
55 on fabric surfaces (Sen, 2007)(Kentta et al., 2022).
56 Foams possess not only significant practical relevance but are also academically interesting as a
57 subject of study. A highly relevant area of research for the process industry is the study of the rheology
58 and flow behaviour of foams (Barigou and Deshpande, 2000) (Herzhaft, 1999) (Gardiner et al., 1999)
59 (Heller and Kuntamukkula, 1987)(Dollet and Raufaste, 2014)(Cohen-Addad and Höhler, 2014).
60 Foams have complex rheological behaviour that can change significantly with foam structure and
61 processing conditions. On the other hand, different foams can also exhibit universal properties. Foams
62 are e.g., generally shear-thinning fluids, and their viscous behaviour can be described with good
63 accuracy with the Herschel–Bulkley model (Princen and Kiss 1986)(Höhler and Cohen-Addad, 2005)

71 The no-slip boundary condition, which holds for most one-phase continuum fluids, does not always
72 hold for foam flow (Denkov et al., 2005) (Briceño and Joseph, 2003)(Marze et al., 2008). This is due
73 to a narrow lubricating pure-liquid layer that develops at the solid walls. A possible explanation for
74 the development of the slip layer is the shear forces dragging out liquid to the wall from the regions
75 between the bubbles (Thondavadi and Lemlich, 1985)(Gardiner et al., 1999). The ensuing slip flow
76 needs to be taken into account when measuring the shear rheology of foams. Slip flow has also
77 practical relevance, as it can increase the flow rate e.g. in pipes significantly. The magnitude of the
78 slip flow depends on the wall shear stress and the foam properties. The slip flow depends also on the
79 roughness of the solid walls (Marze et al., 2008). Strong slip flow has been reported both for glass
80 and acrylic pipes (Jäsberg, Selenius, and Koponen 2015a)(Thondavadi and Lemlich 1985). On the
81 other hand, several papers have reported that slip flow was not seen for steel pipes (Thondavadi and
82 Lemlich, 1985)(Harris and Reidenbach, 1987) (Larmignat et al., 2008). Finally, notice that wall slip
83 depends also on the surface chemistry. Wall slip is higher on hydrophilic surfaces and lower on
84 hydrophobic surfaces (Omirbekov et al., 2022).

85 In the paper and board industry, chemicals are conventionally dosed to the fiber stock before forming.
86 However, foam application of chemicals to the wet web would offer several advantages (Kinnunen-
87 Raudaskoski et al., 2014) (Kenttä et al., 2014)(Kinnunen-Raudaskoski et al., 2017) (Bliss et al., 2021).
88 Strength-additive induced fiber flocculation would be eliminated, and the retention of chemicals on
89 the fiber surfaces would be close to 100% with high dosages and low adsorbing tendencies. Foam
90 application also allows dealing with high-viscosity solutions, and the penetration depth of the applied
91 material to the porous substance can be controlled. Notice that a problem with a close relative of
92 foam-assisted application, spray application, are the creation of mist, limited dosage levels of
93 chemicals due to the difficulty of dealing with highly viscous fluid, the high clogging tendency of the
94 spray nozzles, and problems in obtaining uniform coverage of the base with the sprayed chemical.
95 The current work was part of a public multi-company project where foam application was developed
96 for the paper and board industry (Hjelt et al., 2023)(Oksanen et al., 2023). An important part of this
97 project was the study of the shear rheology, slip flow and pumping of application foams in laboratory
98 and pilot environments. The rheological studies focused on wet foams and the foam density varied
99 between 100 kg/m3 and 300 kg/m3. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVOH) was used as the main surfactant, as it
100 is widely used by the paper and board industry as a strength additive (Gottberg et al., 2019). The
101 dosage of PVOH varied between 0.5% to 6%. Due to the ageing of foam and slip flow, rheological
102 analysis of the application foams was not feasible with traditional rheometer geometries, and a pipe
103 rheometer was used for this purpose (Larmignat et al., 2008) (Enzendorfer et al., 1995) (Ari Jäsberg
104 et al., 2015a). Notice that preliminary results for the PVOH foams, based on a subset of data that
105 constituted one-third of the total dataset, were published in (Jäsberg et al., 2023).
106 The manuscript has been organized in the following way: First, we will introduce the reader to the
107 pipe rheometer, used surfactants and a setup for studying slip flow in opaque pipes. Then we present
108 examples of pressure loss, slip flow and viscosity data in different process conditions and look at the
109 entire dataset. Next, we will perform dimensional analysis for foam viscosity and slip flow to obtain
110 generic formulas that are then used in the analysis of the measurement data. The obtained rheological
111 models are then tested with an experimental surfactant developed for foam application. Then we will
112 present the results of the slip behavior of foam in a smooth steel pipe. Next, we will test the obtained
113 rheological models using a 30 m long pipe with a foam made of a mixture of an experimental
114 surfactant and a widely used dry strength additive. Finally, we will discuss our results, and finish the
115 manuscript in our concludingremarks.
116 2. Experimental
117 Due to their high interfacial free energy, foams are thermodynamically unstable, and the gas and
118 liquid phases tend to gradually separate (Weaire and Hutzler, 1999). This ageing process of the foam
119 is slowed down by the surfactant, but for many foams, it is too rapid for the use of traditional
120 rheometer geometries. Moreover, even with a stable foam, slip flow can be high at the solid walls of
121 the flow geometry and the interpretation of the rheometer results may be challenging (Kroezen et al.,
122 1988) (Marze et al., 2008). For these reasons, foam rheology was studied in this work by using pipe
123 flow. In this case, the foam behaviour can be studied shortly after the foam generation, and the slip
124 flow can be reliably measured. In the literature, several pipe diameters have often been used, and the
125 slip velocity has been estimated by using either the Mooney or Oldrey and Jastrzebski analysis
126 (Enzendorfer et al., 1995)(Gardiner et al., 1998). However, if the pipes are transparent, slip velocity
127 can also be measured explicitly by high-speed imaging (Ari Jäsberg et al., 2015a). In this study, we
128 employed this method. The problem with this approach is that it can be applied directly only to
[[PAGE 3]]
Chemical Engineering Science, autumn 2023, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ces.2023.119282
129 transparent pipes. For this reason, we developed also a setup for measuring the slip flow indirectly in
130 opaque pipes. Notice that the measurement of yield stress is in principle possible with a pipe
131 rheometer (Ari Jäsberg et al., 2015a), but it was left out of the scope of this study. To test the obtained
132 rheological models we performed pressure loss measurements in a 30 m-long pipe.

225 The main surfactant in this study was polyvinyl alcohol (PVOH) Kuraray Poval™ 6-88 (Gottberg et
226 al., 2019). This PVOH grade has a low molecular weight and a degree of hydrolysis in the range of
227 86.7–88.7. The aqueous component was normal tap water, with salinity between 0.2–0.5 mmol/l. The
228 PVOH solutions were prepared by cooking PVOH with tap water at a 10% concentration and diluting
229 the cooled solution to concentrations of 0.5%, 1.0%, 3.0%, or 6.0% by weight. Before measurements,
230 the concentration of each diluted batch was verified in the laboratory. The cooking was started by
231 heating 100 liters of water to the temperature of 50 ºC and adding solid PVOH resin gradually into
232 the water. Next, the temperature was raised to 85 ºC and cooking was continued until the turbidity of
233 the solution disappeared (a few hours). All PVOH solutions were prepared from the same delivered
234 batch of solid resin, and they were stored at room temperature for no more than a few days before
235 being used in the experiment. The pipe rheometer measurements were carried out with an initial
236 PVOH solution in thermal equilibrium with a room temperature of ca. 22°C, and foam density was
237 varied between 100 g/l and 300 g/l with 50 g/l increments. The number of PVOH trial points was
238 966.

260 Dynamic surface tension and dilatational complex modulus measurements
261 We used in this work dynamic surface tension instead of static surface tension as we are dealing with
262 a dynamic process, where the foam is experiencing continuous shearing. The dynamic surface tension
263 of the surfactant solutions was measured by the rising bubble method using a DSA100 drop-shape
264 analyzer (KRÜSS Scientific). A small air bubble (10 μl) was created at the tip of a J-shaped capillary
265 needle inside a temperature-controlled SC30 glass cuvette (KRÜSS Scientific) that was filled with
266 the sample solution. The shape of the air bubble was recorded every 0.1 seconds for 10 minutes, and
267 the surface tension was calculated based on the Young-Laplace equation using the ADVANCE
268 software (v1.12, KRÜSS Scientific). The surface tension measurements were performed at the
269 temperature of 22°C. For the final analysis, surface tension measured att = 0.5 s was used. The choice
270 of the reference time was not critical - the surface tension at e.g. t = 0.1 s and at t = 1.0 s were less
271 than 2% higher and 1% smaller, respectively, than at t = 0.5 s. According to the instrument
272 manufacturer, the accuracy of the surface tension measurement is 0.3 mN/m.

371 Examples of the measured PVOH foam process data
372 We look at some examples of the measured process data in the R = 6.0 mm pipe with a 0.5% PVOH
373 concentration. Foam density varied between 100 kg/m3 and 300 kg/m3. Figure 6 presents loss curves,
374 i.e., pressure drop per unit length of the pipe vs. volumetric flow rate of foam. We see that the viscous
375 pressure loss increases with decreasing foam density (increasing air content). The shape of the loss
376 curves is typical for a shear-thinning fluid: the slope of the curves decreases with an increasing flow
377 rate.
378 Figure 7a shows the slip velocity as a function of wall shear stress. We see that slip increases with
379 increasing wall shear stress. The slip velocity is higher with higher foam density, as it is then easier
380 to drag liquid from the foam to the wall. However, the slope of the curves is similar for the different
381 foam densities. Figure 7b shows that the contribution of the slip flow to the total flow rate can be
382 significant. The contribution is highest at low flow rates and decreases with increasing flow rates.
383 With the smallest flow rates, as much as 70% of the measured flow rate is caused by slip velocity.
384 By applying the Weissenberg-Rabinowitsch correction to the measurement data shown in Figure 6
385 and Figure 7 we got the viscosity-shear rate curves shown in Figure 8. The plotted values are real
386 material parameters without any reference to the used measurements setup (e.g., pipe size or slip
387 flow). We see from Figure 8 that the foam viscosity strongly increases with decreasing foam density.
388 We also see that the relation between the foam viscosity and shear rate is with all the foam densities
389 with a high accuracy a power law, Eq. (2). Moreover, the shear thinning behaviour increases (flow
390 index n decreases) with decreasing foam density.

675 In the literature, it has often been stated that steel pipes have no slip flow. In our measurements, the
676 slip flow of a smooth-walled steel pipe did not differ from the slip of an acrylic pipe. In the earlier
677 studies (Thondavadi and Lemlich, 1985)(Harris and Reidenbach, 1987) (Larmignat et al., 2008) the
678 absence of wall slip in steel pipes was likely caused by the steel pipes having a high surface roughness.
679 In (Thondavadi and Lemlich, 1985), e.g., galvanized steel pipes were used which typically have a
680 roughness of a few hundred micrometres. Notice that for modelling and dimensioning practical
681 processes and for process control the slip flow presents a challenge, as it can be very sensitive to
682 small changes in the process parameters. This problem could be eliminated by using pipes with a high
683 wall roughness at the cost of increasing the pressure loss and potential fouling of the pipe surfaces.
684 In foam application, the applied chemical can be used as the surfactant, as is the case with PVOH.
685 However, usually, the application foam is used as the carrier for applying other chemicals and
[[PAGE 27]]
Chemical Engineering Science, autumn 2023, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ces.2023.119282
686 materials, such as starch or microfibrillated cellulose. We presented one example in our long-pipe
687 experiment where the foam was used to carry a commercial strength additive. In this case, the
688 rheological model worked rather well, but the rheological behaviour of these mixtures can also differ
689 from the carrier foam and should be measured before using them in real-life applications. In (Ari
690 Jäsberg et al., 2015a)(A. Jäsberg et al., 2015b)(Prakash et al., 2021) the addition of cellulosic fibrous
691 materials of various types and sizes up to 5 wt % changed the foam consistency index significantly,
692 but the flow index remained practically unchanged. On the other hand, in (Thondavadi and Lemlich,
693 1985) the addition of small coal and sand particles up to 35 wt % did not change the foam rheology.
694
695 These measurements were performed at atmospheric pressure. In the future, the effect of elevated
696 pressure level on the application foam rheology should be studied to find out when the elevated
697 density of air starts to affect the foam rheology. Another future topic is the effect of temperature on
698 application foam rheology. However, most temperature effects should already be implicitly included
699 in Eqs. (19) and (24) in the solution viscosity, solution surface tension and the foam bubble size.
700 5. Conclusions
701 Foam application of chemicals to the wet web is currently being developed for the paper and board
702 industry. An important part of this work is to understand the rheology of the used application foams.
703 PVOH is widely used as a strength additive in paper and board, and it was chosen as the main
704 surfactant for the foams of this study. The PVOH foam density varied between 100 kg/m3 and 300
705 kg/m3 and the dosage of PVOH varied between 0.5% to 6%.
706 The foam viscosity and slip flow were determined with a pipe rheometer using three pipe diameters.
707 The slip velocity was quantified by recording the foam motion in the vicinity of the wall of an acrylic
708 pipe with a high-speed video camera. A measurement setup was also built for measuring the slip flow
709 directly in opaque pipes.
710 Dimensional analysis was used to obtain general formulas for the foam viscosity and slip flow that
711 were based on several physical quantities describing both the foam and the base liquid. Here the
712 dimensionless shear stress and dimensionless wall shear stress were proportional to certain powers of
713 the capillary number and slip capillary number, respectively. Similar formulas had earlier been
714 obtained by (Denkov et al., 2005) for the air content of 0.9. Our study widens the scope of the models
715 to a much wider air-content region. The obtained rheological models were applied successfully for
716 another surfactant for flow in a short pipe and flow in a long PVC hose using an industrially relevant
717 mixture of carrier foam and a dry strength additive.
718 The contribution of the slip flow to the total flow rate was significant, especially with lower flow
719 rates when most of the volumetric flow was due to the slip. In the literature, many papers have
720 suggested that there is no slip flow in steel pipes. Our results suggest that this is due to the high pipe
721 roughness used in these works. In our measurements, the slip velocity of a smooth-walled steel pipe
722 was identical to an acrylic pipe. This should be taken into account when real-life processes are
723 developed.
724 The obtained viscosity and slip models form a solid basis for developing and running various
725 industrial processes including foam application processes. For new foam recipes, quite a small
726 number of rheological measurements are needed for determining the model parameters for a fixed air
727 content.
