# A thousand baby catfish and the physics of staying together

*Draft for a Chicago urban-ecology or local natural-history outlet. ~2,000 words.
Submission notes at the end.*

---

At the far end of Montrose Pier, where the concrete bends back on itself into a rough J,
there is a pocket of shallow water that most people jog straight past. On the eighth of
August I stopped there, a little after noon, and looked down into it.

The bottom was pale sand and silt with ragged patches of green filamentous algae, all of it
perfectly visible through maybe two feet of water. And moving across it were two dark shapes
that I first read as shadows. They were not shadows. They were fish, thousands of them, so
tightly packed that from above they looked like a single object with a skin.

I stood there for four minutes with my phone out. I have thought about those four minutes
almost every day since.

## What they were

Baby catfish. Bullheads, genus *Ameiurus*, at the stage where they have absorbed their yolk
sacs and lifted off the nest but are nowhere near ready to be on their own.

This is a documented and rather wonderful piece of natural history. Bullheads are among the
minority of North American freshwater fish that guard their young after hatching. The
Michigan Department of Natural Resources describes the behaviour in language that matches
what I was looking at almost exactly: small clouds of bullhead fry travel the shoreline
while the parents circle the school to keep them together, for roughly two weeks. If a fry
strays, a parent will collect it in its mouth and carry it back.

So this dark ball is not a leaderless democracy of equals. It is a herded group, with an
adult somewhere nearby whose entire job is preventing it from coming apart. I should say
plainly that I never saw the parent. I have looked through the frames and there is no adult
fish in any of them. It may have been below the school, or off to the side, or the brood may
have been at the stage where the guarding has already lapsed. What I can say is that the
behaviour of the young matches the guarded-brood stage precisely.

I also cannot tell you which bullhead. Black bullhead and brown bullhead both occur here and
both look identical from directly overhead at fry size. One photograph of a single fry in the
hand would settle it, and I intend to get one.

## Why a physicist would care

There is a famous piece of science about starlings that this connects to, and it is the
reason I could not let the videos sit on my phone.

For years, Giorgio Parisi, Andrea Cavagna, Irene Giardina and their colleagues in Rome
pointed synchronised cameras at the enormous starling flocks that gather over the city at
dusk. They reconstructed the position and velocity of every individual bird, thousands at a
time, and then asked a question borrowed from statistical physics. If one bird deviates from
the flock's average heading, how far away can you go and still find birds deviating the same
way?

The answer turned out to be: as far as the flock is wide. Whatever the size of the flock,
the range of coordination scaled up to match it. In physics this is called scale-free
correlation, and it is a signature of a system poised near a critical point. It means each
bird's *influence* travels much further than its eyesight does, which is how a flock of
thousands can swerve away from a peregrine as though it were one animal.

That paper (Parisi won a Nobel Prize for other work, but this is the one that got into
people's imaginations) reframed flocking as a physics problem. Since then the same
mathematics has been applied to midge swarms, to bacteria, to sheep, to crowds.

Fish schools have been studied intensively too, but almost entirely in laboratory tanks. The
standard subject is the golden shiner, a freshwater minnow, in the hands of Iain Couzin's
group. Their work established that schooling fish sit in at least three distinct and stable
collective states: a disordered *swarm* that is cohesive but internally aimless, a *mill* in
which the group rotates like a slow torus, and a *polarised* school where everyone travels
the same direction together.

What I could not find, and I looked, is anyone doing this arithmetic on catfish. Or on any
ictalurid fry ball. The behaviour is in the natural-history literature as description. It
does not appear to be in the physics literature as measurement. I hold that conclusion
loosely, because the older fisheries journals are badly indexed and this is exactly where
such work would hide.

## What four minutes of phone video can actually tell you

Quite a lot, with one enormous caveat I want to get out of the way first.

There is no scale in these videos. No ruler, no calibration target, and a camera height that
changed every time my arm moved. I cannot tell you how big these schools were in centimetres,
and any number I gave you would be invented.

What I can do is measure the fish and use them as the ruler. Every length in what follows is
in body lengths: one fry. It turns out this is not a consolation prize. If my arm drifted
lower, the school and the fry both got bigger in the image by the same factor, so every ratio
survived the drift that I could not otherwise correct for. The four schools gave the same fry
size to within five percent of each other, which is a decent sign that the measurement is
tracking the animals rather than my hands.

For a sense of physical scale: published accounts put bullhead fry at this stage around 20 to
30 mm long. If that holds, these schools were roughly two to three feet across. That estimate
comes from the literature, not from my footage, and nothing else here depends on it.

So, the numbers.

**The schools were 30 to 40 fry across**, packed densely enough to block 78 to 86 percent of
the view of the bottom beneath them. They were not round. Their outlines ran a quarter to
forty percent longer than a circle of the same area, which is a precise way of saying the
edges were shaggy, with individuals constantly loitering off the boundary.

**All three collective states showed up, in four minutes.** One clip is a disordered swarm.
Two are polarised schools travelling together. One is milling, rotating coherently around its
own centre. Two of those clips have nearly identical alignment scores and are doing
completely different things, which is a good argument for measuring rotation as well as
alignment. If I had only measured how aligned the fish were, I would have called a rotating
mill "disordered" and been wrong.

Seeing the whole known repertoire inside four minutes at one spot on one afternoon was not
what I expected.

**The middle of the school was more organised than the edge.** In every one of the four
clips, without exception, fish in the interior were better aligned with each other than fish
around the rim. This is the sort of result that means more for being boringly consistent.

**And the schools split and rejoined constantly.** I counted eleven separation episodes.
Every single one of them ended in reunion. Eleven for eleven. Over three minutes of usable
footage, no piece of a school was ever permanently lost.

These are not a few stragglers peeling away, either. At the deepest point of a typical split,
the larger piece held only 58 to 74 percent of the fish, so the group was genuinely coming
apart into comparable halves and then finding its way back together. The separations lasted
anywhere from a sixth of a second to nearly a minute, and the longest of them took up most
of one clip.

The single most striking sequence in the footage runs the other way. Two entirely separate
schools converge over about five seconds, and just before their outlines touch you can see a
thin bridge of individual fish streaming across the gap between them, as though the merger
propagates through a few volunteers first. Then they are one school.

## Where the fish part company with the birds

Here is where I have to report a result I did not want.

I measured the range of coordination in these schools, using the same quantity Cavagna's
group used on the starlings. The coordination is real. Compared against a control in which I
shuffled the fish velocities randomly among their positions, the measured range is far larger
than chance in every clip.

But it does not scale with the size of the school. In the starlings, bigger flock meant
proportionally longer reach, every time. In these fry balls the reach stays roughly fixed at
a few body lengths regardless of how big the school is. If anything it drifts slightly
downward with size.

I want to be careful about how much weight that carries. My four schools only span a factor
of 1.8 in size, and you cannot confidently fit a scaling law across less than a doubling.
This is a hint, not a finding. But it is a hint in the opposite direction from the birds, and
that is interesting enough to be worth someone's afternoon.

There is a tidy possible explanation, and I have no evidence for it. Starling flocks are
self-organised among equals. A guarded fry ball has a parent imposing cohesion from outside.
A group that is being actively herded does not need long-range internal coordination to stay
together, because something else is doing that job. Whether that is actually why, I cannot
say from four minutes of video.

## The mistake I almost published

One methodological confession, because I think it generalises to anyone who might try this
with a phone.

My first pass at measuring how well aligned the fish were produced beautiful numbers. In one
stretch the school scored 0.998 out of a possible 1.0, which would be essentially perfect
collective alignment, the kind of result you would build a paper around.

It was my arm. The camera was panning at speed, and when the camera pans, every point in the
frame moves the same direction at the same rate. An alignment statistic cannot tell the
difference between a thousand fish swimming in formation and a photographer turning slowly to
the left. Once I estimated the camera's motion from the stationary lake bed and subtracted it,
that same moment scored 0.171.

Across the footage, about a third of frames were meaningfully inflated this way. The median
inflation was small, which is the trap: if I had checked only the average I would have
concluded the correction did not matter and kept a result that was mostly a picture of my own
hand.

## What I would do differently, and what anyone could do

Three things would turn this from a curiosity into something defensible, and none of them
require equipment.

Photograph one fry against a ruler. Five minutes, and it retroactively converts every
measurement here into centimetres.

Wedge the phone against the railing instead of holding it. That removes the single largest
source of error in the whole project.

And film more schools, especially different sized ones, because the one genuinely
interesting question here (does coordination scale with group size, as in birds, or not, as
these four hint) is limited entirely by having only four schools that were all about the same
size.

I keep coming back to how ordinary the setting was. This is not a remote field site. It is a
pier where people fish for perch and photograph warblers and walk their dogs, in a city of
2.7 million, a few hundred metres from Lake Shore Drive. The behaviour has been described in
agency pamphlets for decades. Nobody, as far as I can tell, has ever bothered to measure it.
It took a phone, an afternoon, and being willing to stop running.
