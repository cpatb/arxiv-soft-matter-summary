# F2 — what the `φ_J` shift is actually worth

**What is plotted.** The ratio `η(high CV) / η(low CV)` under the Wyart–Cates form
`η ∝ (φ_J − φ)^(−2)`, as a function of the packing fraction `φ` at which the suspension is
used. The dotted horizontal line is unity (no effect); the dashed vertical line is the
lab's operating point, `φ = 0.44`.

**Why the ratio is below one.** `φ_J` *rises* with CV (F1), so at fixed `φ` a polydisperse
suspension sits *further* from jamming than a monodisperse one and is therefore **less**
viscous. Polydispersity thins. That is the direction of the bench observation this project
was built to explain, and it means the ratio runs toward zero as `φ` approaches
`φ_J(low CV)` — never toward infinity.

⚠ The first version of this figure plotted the reciprocal and asked where the effect
reached a factor of +10. That question has no answer: it returned `φ = 0.672`, above both
`φ_J` values and therefore unreachable by construction. The sign of the pre-registered test
was wrong, not the data.

**What it shows.** The effect is real but weak where the lab works. At `φ = 0.44` the
`φ_J` shift buys a factor of roughly 0.65–0.77 — a few tens of percent, not a decade. One
decade of thinning needs `φ ≳ 0.62`, which is close to `φ_J` itself.

**What it does not show.** The *local* packing fraction inside an impacting drop is not the
bulk 0.44 — the material is dynamically compacted at the contact. This figure says what the
shift is worth at a given `φ`; it does not say which `φ` the impact actually reaches. That
is the measurement that would settle whether M2 matters in the experiment.
