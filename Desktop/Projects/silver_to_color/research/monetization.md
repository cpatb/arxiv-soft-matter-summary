# Monetization: AI-restored public-domain film on YouTube

Research date **2026-08-25/26**. Every rate, threshold and policy quote below carries a source
link and the date it was checked. Where sources disagree, both are given. Where nothing
authoritative exists, it says so instead of guessing.

Tags used throughout:

- **[OFFICIAL]**: platform help centre, legal terms, or newsroom page, fetched directly
- **[PRIMARY-LEGAL]**: statute, court opinion, Federal Register, or Copyright Office publication
- **[TRADE]**: reputable trade press
- **[BLOG]**: creator-tool or SEO blog; directional only
- **[GAP]**: could not be verified; stated as a hole rather than filled in

A warning that applies to this whole topic: search results for creator monetization are
saturated with AI-written blogspam that recycles the same unsourced numbers between pages. A
figure repeated on twenty sites is not corroborated, it is copy-pasted. Anything marked [BLOG]
below is a rough direction, not a number to plan against.

## Part 1: the one thing that decides everything else

Before any revenue figure matters, one question governs the whole project:

> **Will YouTube's reviewers accept an AI-restoration channel into the Partner Program?**

The answer is not published, and it is worth understanding exactly why.

### 1.1 Public-domain status buys you nothing here

This is the most-misunderstood point in the archival niche, and YouTube states it explicitly.
From the reused content policy
([support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?p=reused_content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?p=reused_content&hl=en),
checked 2026-08-25) **[OFFICIAL]**:

> Reused content "is separate from YouTube's Copyright enforcement, which means it's not based
> on copyright, permission, or fair use. This policy applies even if you have permission from
> the original creator."

Being legally free to use a 1906 film and being monetization-eligible for it are unrelated
questions. Every archival channel has to clear the transformation bar on its own merits, and a
clean copyright position contributes nothing to that.

### 1.2 The governing test, and the two policies that bite

The reused content policy defines the problem as channels that "repurpose content that's already
on YouTube or another online source without adding significant original commentary, substantive
modifications, or educational or entertainment value." Permission is granted where "viewers can
tell that there's a meaningful difference between the original video and your video."

On a literal reading, a colorized and upscaled 1906 film passes that test easily; the difference
is the most visible thing about the video. That is a genuinely strong argument and it is worth
making.

The counter-argument is that reviewers have historically read "substantive modifications" as an
*editorial* contribution (what you say about the footage) rather than a *processing* contribution
(what you ran the footage through). Nothing in the published text settles which reading applies.

Two verbatim bullets frame the risk precisely:

| Side | Bullet |
|---|---|
| Allowed | "Edited footage with audio and visual effects on top of the video's reused content that demonstrates substantive editing" |
| Not allowed | "Content downloaded or copied from another online source without any substantive modifications" |

**The second policy is the sharper one and it is routinely overlooked.** Separate from reused
content, the *Generic or repetitive content* section (formerly "repetitious," renamed
"inauthentic" on 2025-07-15, restructured again around July 2026) disallows:

> "Similar or repetitive content with low educational value, commentary, narratives, or minimal
> variation"

and

> "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates giving the impression of mass
> production without adding the creator's original, authentic insights or perspective"

while allowing "similar content ... in which each video has a distinct storyline, focus, or
concept."

A back catalogue of *1920s street scene, colorized, upscaled, no narration* is format-identical
video to video. The source film changes; the creator's contribution does not. **A restoration
channel can clear the reused-content bar and still fail the repetitive-content bar.** The first
asks whether you changed the source enough, the second asks whether your channel is a template.

Enforcement on both is **channel-level and human**: "If you have videos that violate these
guidelines, or if YouTube cannot clearly tell that you made the content, monetization may be
removed from your entire channel."

Source for all quotes:
[support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en),
checked 2026-08-25 **[OFFICIAL]**. One documentation quirk worth knowing: the page footer still
reads "July 15, 2025" while the section names reflect a mid-2026 restructure reported by
[Tubefilter (2026-07-13)](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/07/13/youtube-inauthentic-content-monetization-policy-update/)
and [TechCrunch (2026-07-20)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/youtube-clarifies-policies-around-ai-slop-and-upsetting-videos/)
**[TRADE]**. Both report the rules themselves did not change. Treat the section names as live and
the footer date as stale.

### 1.3 What maps onto the allowed-side language

YouTube has never published anything about restoration, colorization, before/after formats, or
archival footage. Anyone claiming YouTube endorses the before/after format is extrapolating. So
is the table below. The difference is that it is anchored to verbatim allowed-side bullets.

| Format element | Nearest official allowed-side language | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Spoken narration** | Appears in five of six allowed bullets, "explain the moves," "you comment on the original," "add a storyline and commentary," "rewritten the dialog and changed the voiceover," "critical review" | **Strongest by a distance** |
| **Before/after in-frame** | "viewers can tell that there's a meaningful difference"; "substantive editing ... unique to your channel" | Moderate; structurally elegant, because it makes the transformation visible to a reviewer in seconds |
| **On-screen historical context** | "add a storyline and commentary"; "educational value" | Moderate, but "scrolling text with minimal or no narrative" is explicitly *not allowed*, so text alone is insufficient |
| **Restoration alone, unnarrated** | Sits between "substantive editing" and "without any substantive modifications" | **This is the coin-flip.** Undecided, discretionary, human-judged |

The convergent read across creator-education sources matches the policy text: **commentary is
the spine and footage is the support, not the reverse.** The most useful of these is Mediacube,
which quotes its own Head of Channel Review, Olga Karpovich, saying review happens "at the
channel level, not just video by video" and that "there are no rules or numbers"
([mediacube.io, published 2026-06-19](https://mediacube.io/en-US/blog/youtube-reused-content),
checked 2026-08-25) **[BLOG, but first-hand]**. Several such sources warn specifically that
"adding basic filters or AI motion to someone else's footage" does not by itself create
sufficient value. That warning is aimed at low-effort AI channels, but it lands uncomfortably
close to an unnarrated restoration channel, and the resemblance is worth taking seriously rather
than explaining away.

### 1.4 AI use is not the problem; unnarrated repetition is

There is **no blanket ban on AI content and no automatic demonetization for using AI tools**.
The policy targets output, not tooling: not monetizable is "AI-generated content made with
generic or unoriginal templates," while "content that utilizes creative tools to assist in
delivering a unique, well-researched, or creative narrative" is fine.

One category is permanently off-limits and is easy to wander into: channels using "AI-generated
personas to deliver information on sensitive topics" (health, legal, financial, political) are
never monetizable. Practical consequence, do not build an AI narrator presenting as a historian
who gives advice. An AI *voice* reading your own script is a different thing from an AI *persona*,
but keeping that distinction visible is your job, not the reviewer's.

### 1.5 Disclosure is free, so disclose

From [Disclosing use of altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
(checked 2026-08-25) **[OFFICIAL]**:

**Exempt from disclosure**, "Video sharpening, upscaling or repair", "Color adjustment or
lighting filters", "Special effects like ... vintage effects", captions, and generative AI used
"for production assistance" such as scripts or ideas.

**Requires disclosure**, content where AI is "altering footage of real events or places", makes
a real person appear to do something they did not, or "generates a realistic scene that didn't
actually occur."

**The colorization gap is genuinely unresolved.** Upscaling is exempt by name. Colorization is
not addressed anywhere. It sits between an exempt bullet (color adjustment) and a triggering one
(altering footage of real events and places), and 1906 street footage is, definitionally,
footage of a real place. No YouTube page, blog post, or credible secondary source resolves it.
The sources do not disagree; they are silent.

The decision is easy anyway, because the cost is asymmetric. The same help page states:

> "Disclosing AI content won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money."

while creators who "consistently" fail to disclose face "manual application of a label, or
penalties from YouTube, including removal of content or suspension from the YouTube Partner
Program."

Zero cost to disclose, channel-level risk if you guess wrong. **Disclose, and put a plain
sentence in the description too**, "the colors are an AI reconstruction, not the original
photography." That line also does double duty as the educational-value argument in §1.3.

### 1.6 Content ID: the claims that look false, and the ones that only look false

Public-domain material is formally **ineligible** as a Content ID reference. From
[Content eligible for Content ID](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2605065) (checked
2026-08-25) **[OFFICIAL]**, the not-eligible list includes verbatim "Public domain footage,
recordings, or compositions," and eligibility requires "exclusive rights to the material."

On paper nobody may claim a 1906 film. In practice three distinguishable things happen:

1. **Genuinely improper claims**, a rightsholder uploads a modern reissue as a reference without
   excising the PD footage inside it, and the system then matches everyone's copy of the
   underlying film. The canonical illustration is Universal's claim over public-domain footage
   posted by librarian Michael Sauers
   ([Techdirt, 2019](https://www.techdirt.com/articles/20190420/23485242053/emilio-estevez-uses-some-public-domain-footage-film-so-universal-studios-forces-original-public-domain-footage-offline.shtml))
   **[TRADE]**. Old case, unchanged mechanism.
2. **Music claims that are entirely valid**, a modern score on a restored silent film is a live
   copyright in both composition and recording. If you pull a restoration carrying a modern score,
   the music claim is correct even though the film is PD.
3. **Asserted restoration authorship**, restorers claim rights in their restoration work as a new
   contribution (e.g. Lobster Films' 2016 *The Lost World*). Whether restoration attracts its own
   copyright is contested and jurisdiction-dependent; US originality doctrine after *Bridgeman* is
   unfriendly to it, EU law is more permissive. **Unsettled**, see §5.3.

**Two practical rules follow.** Source the rawest available scan rather than someone else's
polished restoration, and never inherit a modern soundtrack, mute it and score it yourself. Both
reduce claim exposure and strengthen the originality case at the same time.

Disputing is not free. "It's in the public domain" is **not one of the enumerated dispute
reasons** on [Dispute a Content ID claim](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797454?hl=en)
(checked 2026-08-25) **[OFFICIAL]**, you file under "misidentified or an error was made." The
claimant then has 30 days and may release, reinstate, let it expire, or escalate to a takedown
that puts a **copyright strike** on the channel. A strike is disqualifying for YPP. You cannot
cancel a dispute once filed. Keep per-video provenance records, archive.org item URL, stated
rights status, scan date, film release year, so a dispute can cite a specific ineligible
reference instead of asserting PD status in the abstract. The `tools/make_metadata.py` script
in this project emits that record automatically.

## Part 2: YPP thresholds, and a deadline that halves the work

### 2.1 Current thresholds

**Main tier** (ad revenue + Premium share), 
[support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851?hl=en),
checked 2026-08-25 **[OFFICIAL]**. Meet one of:

| Route | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Long-form | **1,000 subscribers + 4,000 qualified public watch hours** in the last 12 months |
| Shorts | **1,000 subscribers + 10,000,000 qualified Shorts views** in the last 90 days |

Plus all of: available country, 2-Step Verification on, advanced features access, a linked
AdSense account, and no active Community Guidelines strikes. The **advanced features** requirement
is easy to miss and needs separate ID or video verification, start it early, it is not instant.

**Lower "expanded" tier** (fan funding only, memberships, Super Thanks, Super Chat, Shopping;
**not** ad revenue), 
[support.google.com/youtube/answer/13429240](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13429240?hl=en),
checked 2026-08-25 **[OFFICIAL]**. Meet one of:

| Route | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Long-form | **500 subscribers + 3 valid public uploads in 90 days + 3,000 watch hours** in 12 months |
| Shorts | **500 subscribers + 3 valid public uploads in 90 days + 3,000,000 Shorts views** in 90 days |

Review turnaround is stated as about a month, longer when volume is high.

### 2.2 The thresholds double on 2027-02-01

Sources: [blog.youtube, published 2026-08-10](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/youtube-partner-program-updates-2027-new-opportunities-earn/)
and [Changes to the YouTube Partner Program](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12843009?hl=en),
both checked 2026-08-25 **[OFFICIAL]**.

From **2027-02-01**, new applicants must meet **1,000 subscribers + 8,000 qualified watch hours
in 365 days**, or **20,000,000 Shorts views in 90 days**. Both routes double. **Existing YPP
members are grandfathered.** The 500-sub fan-funding tier keeps its current numbers.

Also on that page: Shorts monetization becomes an ongoing gate (10M qualified Shorts views per
rolling 90 days, falling below suspends Shorts earnings only, it does not eject you from YPP);
a channel activity requirement; and an **acceptance deadline of 2027-01-31** for updated
monetization module terms. Premium Lite is rolling out with a 60% net creator share, split
55% long-form / 45% Shorts.

**This is the most schedule-relevant fact in this document.** Being *accepted*, not merely
applying, before 2027-02-01 is worth roughly half the effort of qualifying after. With a review
queue of about a month, the effective deadline is closer to the end of December 2026. That is
roughly four months out, and it should set the publishing cadence.

## Part 3: what ads actually pay

### 3.1 The number

**$5-12 RPM, centred on $7-9**, for English-narrated history with a US/UK-majority audience.

Anchors behind that range, all creator self-reports (2025-26): **$8.10** at 97% US and 45+
audience; **$9.09** for a "US male boomer" audience; **$5-8** for French-language history;
**$12** for 20-45 minute videos carrying two to three mid-rolls.

**Confidence: moderate.** This rests on roughly a dozen self-reports, most from a single April
2026 thread, with no verified analytics screenshots. Treat it as the right order of magnitude and
the right shape, not as a forecast.

### 3.2 The finding that should change what you make

**Silent archival footage is language-neutral, which sounds like an advantage and is the opposite
of one.** With no spoken language, the algorithm serves it worldwide, and a globally distributed
audience destroys RPM: **€1.87** reported for a Spanish-language audience, **$0.90** at 50% India.

**Expect $1.50-4 RPM for unnarrated restoration content, versus $7-9 narrated.** That is a
**3-5x revenue difference driven entirely by whether you speak over the footage.**

This is the single most useful thing in the monetization research, because it converges with
everything else. Narration is:

- the reused-content policy's most reliably cited transformation (§1.3),
- the fix for the generic/repetitive-content risk (§1.2),
- TikTok's 60-second minimum, satisfied naturally (§4.2),
- Meta's originality carve-out (§4.3),
- and a 3-5x RPM multiplier.

Five independent pressures, one answer. **Narrate the videos.**

than from third-party stats sites.

| Rick88888888 | 250K | Yes | Restores from source |
| Michael Rogge | 551K | Yes | Filmed much of his own footage |
| Periscope Film | 902K | Yes | 11,398 near-raw scans of prints it physically owns |

**Two corrections to the channel list in the brief.** "Guy Jones", **could not verify**; no live

Of the eleven channels checked, **none had memberships enabled** and three had Patreon.

### 3.4 What separates approved from rejected

**The honest headline: the direct evidence is thin.** I found **zero first-hand YPP accounts from
anyone applying with AI-colorized archival film specifically.** That absence is itself the
finding, and it is why §1.2 treats the question as genuinely undecided rather than resolved.

From adjacent cases, the separating axis looks like **provenance rather than effort**:

- Periscope Film posts near-raw scans of prints **it physically owns** and is monetized.
- Michael Rogge filmed much of his own footage.
- Meanwhile, a bodycam channel with "narration, editing, motion graphics, storytelling,
  commentary" was **rejected, and its appeal denied**.
- And a musician was **ejected from YPP partly over Prelinger public-domain footage**, the same
  archive as the first film on your list. Worth knowing before you build the channel on it.

The policy language explains why clearance does not help: the test is content "already on YouTube
or **another online source**." An archive.org scan is another online source, whoever owns the
print.

**The rejection wording is the most instructive artifact found** (2026):

> "Appeal didn't demonstrate enough value-add editing. The editing shown in your appeal didn't
> substantially change the original work."

Note what failed there: the appellant submitted **an editing timelapse and Premiere project
files**. **Proving labour does not work. On-screen difference does.** A reviewer with seconds and
no context needs to *see* the transformation, which is the concrete argument for putting the
before/after in the frame early (§1.3) and for narrating over it.

**NASS complicates the pattern and should not be smoothed away.** It is a pure AI
restoration/colorization channel, started in 2020, running ads at 787K subscribers, with no
physical ownership of anything. Its existence is the strongest available evidence that this can
work. One verified example is not a rule, but it is considerably better than the vendor blogs
asserting the same conclusion with nothing behind it.

### 3.5 The structural levers

Two factors move a channel within the RPM band far more than the niche label:

1. **Audience geography**, see §3.2. This is the dominant term, and narration is how you control
   it.
2. **Video length and ad load.** Past 8 minutes you can run mid-rolls; the $12 anchor above is a
   20-45 minute video with two to three of them. Longer, narrated pieces are the whole game.

## Part 4: short-form is a funnel, not a revenue line

Full working notes: `_raw/shortform.md` in the research scratch (not committed).

### 4.1 YouTube Shorts

Shorts revenue sits inside YPP; there is no separate fund (the Creator Fund was retired in 2023).
Mechanically, from [Shorts monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12504220?hl=en)
(checked 2026-08-25) **[OFFICIAL]**: ads shown between Shorts pool into a pot, money is set aside
for music licensing, what remains is the **Creator Pool** allocated by share of engaged views,
and the creator keeps **45%**.

**Music licensing is deducted before the split, and it is brutal.** Verbatim: with one music
track, "half of the revenue associated with its engaged views would be allocated to the Creator
Pool"; with two tracks, "one third." **A library-music score costs you 50-67% of that Short's
contribution.** Given that the RPM is already pennies, this is one of the few 2x levers available, use voiceover or self-composed/PD score and keep the whole contribution.

Note also that payment is on **engaged views**, and the explicitly ineligible list includes
"Non-original Shorts, such as unedited clips from others' movies or TV shows ... or compilations
with no original content added."

Because payout is proportional to your share of *all* monetizing Shorts that month, the model is
structurally deflationary: if Shorts supply grows faster than Shorts ad revenue, your RPM falls
even when your views are flat.

**Reported RPM** (no official figure exists, YouTube publishes none):

| Source | Type | Date | Shorts RPM per 1,000 views |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Mediacube](https://mediacube.io/en-US/blog/youtube-shorts-rpm) | [BLOG] MCN | 2026-01-30 | **$0.01-$0.05** |
| [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-monetization/) | [BLOG] vendor | 2026, undated body | $0.03-$0.10 |
| Aggregated consensus | [BLOG] | 2026 | $0.02-$0.12 |

**The arithmetic that should govern the decision:** 1,000,000 Shorts views per month at $0.05 RPM
is **$50/month**. The Shorts-only YPP gate is 10M views in 90 days, **you can clear the
monetization threshold and still earn under $100/month.**

### 4.2 TikTok

The Creator Rewards Program (formerly Creativity Program Beta). From the EEA terms document
([tiktok.com/legal/...creator-rewards-program-eea](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/tiktok-creator-rewards-program-eea/en),
"Last Updated: Apr 2025", checked 2026-08-25) **[OFFICIAL]**, note this is the EEA version, the
only one that rendered as fetchable text; the US terms page and the entire Creator Academy are
JavaScript-rendered and could not be read directly.

Eligibility: **10,000 authentic followers**, **100,000 authentic video views in the last 30 days**,
18+, personal (not business) account, resident of an available country with no VPN. Available
countries: US, UK, Germany, France, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Brazil.

Two rules reshape the product:

- **Videos must be one minute or longer.** A before/after reveal is naturally 15-20 seconds. To
  earn on TikTok you must build a 60s+ piece, multiple restorations, or one restoration with real
  narration. **This is not a tweak, it changes what you are making.** It also happens to be the
  change that most improves your originality position on every platform.
- **Photo Mode video is excluded outright.** Do not build these as slideshows of stills.

**Qualified views** are the catch nobody accounts for: "those that a user views for five seconds
or more, are not marked by the viewer as 'not interested'", from an eligible country, and search
traffic counts only past 30 seconds. Creator reports put the qualification rate around 40-70%, so
**effective RPM against your public view counter is roughly half the headline.**

Eligibility for this content type turns on one clause, content must be "original content and
produced entirely by the creator **and/or adds new ideas to preexisting content**." A restoration
is definitionally an addition to preexisting content, and that clause is a real opening.

The risk is not the policy, it is the classifier. TikTok's unoriginality detection has a
well-documented false-positive problem, and **archival footage is close to the worst possible
input for it**, the same public-domain reels sit on hundreds of accounts, so a fingerprint match
will find duplicates of your *source* even when your treatment is original. Originality is also a
**scoring input**, not just a pass/fail gate, so a partially-original video can be paid less
rather than rejected.

**Reported pay: $0.40-$1.00 per 1,000 qualified views**, trending to the low end, ≈ **$200-$500
per million public views**. Minimum payout $50/month. That is **5-10x YouTube Shorts per view**,
which is the clearest single finding in the short-form research.

**US ban risk is resolved.** The US joint venture closed 2026-01-22 with American entities holding
80.1% and ByteDance 19.9%; Oracle, Silver Lake and MGX hold 15% each; the algorithm is secured in
Oracle's US cloud; Executive Order 14352 (2025-09-25) determined the JV a "qualified divestiture."
([Fox Business, 2026-01-22](https://www.foxbusiness.com/technology/tiktok-avoids-us-ban-finalizing-historic-trump-backed-american-majority-ownership-deal),
[Variety](https://variety.com/2025/digital/news/tiktok-us-joint-venture-deal-close-date-oracle-silver-lake-1236612315/))
**[TRADE]**. Investing effort in TikTok is no longer an existential bet.

### 4.3 Instagram

**There is no per-view payment available to a general creator, and this is the headline.** Reels
Play Bonus ended in March 2023 and nothing replaced it
([TechCrunch, 2023-03-10](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/10/meta-will-stop-offering-reels-bonuses-to-creators-on-facebook-and-instagram/))
**[TRADE]**. Instagram's own bonuses page says, verbatim: **"Bonuses are limited time and
invite-only as we build toward a sustainable program."**
([creators.instagram.com/earn-money/bonuses](https://creators.instagram.com/earn-money/bonuses),
checked 2026-08-25) **[OFFICIAL]**. There is no application, no published rate, no threshold.

**A correction worth making explicitly:** several blogs claim Instagram shares 55% of Reels ad
revenue and pays a $1-$5 RPM. No official Meta or Instagram page describes any such program, and
`creators.instagram.com/earn-money` does not list one. The 55% figure is the long-standing
**Facebook** in-stream rate, apparently copied across by aggregators. **Treat "Instagram pays
$1-$5 RPM on Reels" as unsubstantiated.**

What does exist: Gifts (creator receives ~$0.01 per Star **[BLOG]**), Subscriptions (Instagram
takes 0%, but app stores take ~30%, use web links and keep ~97%), Badges, and brand-deal tooling.
These are audience-monetization tools with no originality gate.

**The binding risk on Instagram is reach, not payment.** Meta's originality regime deprioritizes
"limited originality" content that is "principally repurposed from other sources," with penalties
including reduced reach and non-recommendable account status
([Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/enforcement/taking-action/lowering-distribution-of-problematic-content/)).
Meta's March 2026 announcement on rewarding original creators
([about.fb.com, 2026-03-13](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/rewarding-original-creators-on-facebook/))
**[OFFICIAL]** explicitly deprioritizes "stitching multiple clips together, or narrating what's
already on screen" and minor edits such as borders, captions and speed changes. **Scope caveat:
that announcement is Facebook-specific and does not name Instagram**, but Meta has said its
duplicate-detection tooling already scans both.

Two hard rules for this content type: **never post a clip carrying another archive's watermark**
(Meta names visible watermarks as a clear unoriginality signal), and **put your voice in it**
(the "original" carve-out is built around creator presence adding analysis).

### 4.4 Cross-platform reality check

At **one million views per month per platform**, posting the same asset everywhere:

| Platform | Realistic monthly | Note |
|---|---|---|
| YouTube Shorts | **$10-$100** | Halve it if you use library music |
| TikTok | **$200-$500** | Only if every video is 60s+ and passes originality scoring |
| Instagram Reels | **~$0** | Plus whatever Gifts the audience chooses to send |

**Roughly 3M views/month across all three plausibly yields $250-$600/month before tax**, at a
full-time posting cadence. Anyone quoting materially more is citing US-only finance-niche RPMs or
selling something.

### 4.5 The verdict, and the synergy that makes it easy

**Short-form is marketing spend with a small rebate. Budget it that way.**

Long-form history RPM runs roughly 40-300x the Shorts rate. The same restoration, cut as a
12-minute narrated piece instead of a 20-second reveal, earns more from 20,000 views than the
Short earns from a million. Short-form's real value is different and genuine: **algorithmic
distribution that does not require an existing audience**, a cold-start discovery channel.

A caution on the funnel statistics circulating: claims like "channels using Shorts + long-form
grow 41% faster" and "3-5x subscriber conversion" trace to blog pages with no methodology, no
sample and no dated study, recycling each other. **Do not put those numbers in a plan.** The one
study with an actual sample (AIR Media-Tech, 274 channels,
[air.io](https://air.io/en/air-data-findings/youtube-shorts-rpm-vs-long-form-how-much-do-shorts-earn-in-2026),

of long-form; short-form distribution works from a standing start; and before/after restoration is
unusually well-suited to *both*, the reveal is a perfect 8-second hook, and the provenance,
history and methodology are a perfect 15-minute video. Few niches have that property.

**The most important structural insight in this whole document:** the fix for the policy risk and
the fix for the revenue problem are the same fix. A narrated restoration piece with historical
context, cut down into short-form teasers, simultaneously clears YouTube's "significant original
commentary" bar, clears TikTok's 60-second minimum and its "adds new ideas" clause, clears Meta's
creator-presence carve-out, and creates the long-form asset where the money actually is.

**Ranked:** TikTok first for direct short-form revenue (5-10x Shorts, ban risk resolved), YouTube
Shorts second and explicitly as a funnel, Instagram third for audience and brand-deal surface
only. **Use no library music anywhere**, it halves YouTube revenue and adds an unoriginality
signal on TikTok and Meta.

### 5.1 Commissioned restorations, the only month-one revenue

**Consumer film digitization, published per-foot rates:** Larsen Digital $0.25-0.30/ft; Capture
(Costco's fulfiller) $0.30/ft after a $34.98/100ft floor; ScanCafe $0.34/ft; Boutique Transfer
"from $0.35/ft"; Legacybox uses $70-$1,250 flat kit pricing that hides the per-foot cost.
DigMyPics has **shut down** (stated on their own site).

- SD/HD 8mm / Super 8 / 16mm consensus band: **$0.25-0.35/ft**
- 4K tier: **$0.45-0.65/ft**
- Sound adds **$0.10-0.25/ft**

**Archival-grade scanning runs 2-7x consumer rates:** Archival Works $0.45-0.72/ft; Film
Preservation Lab $0.42-2.35/ft by tier with a $150 minimum; CinePost professional 16mm
$0.25-0.45/ft. Colorlab, DuArt, Metropolis Post and Gamma Ray Digital publish nothing, which is
itself informative. Archival work is quoted per job after inspection, not off a rate card.

**Restoration pricing is the opaque segment.** The few published anchors: **CinePost at $195/hour**
for scene-to-scene DaVinci color correction (the one clean hourly anchor in the market); Media
Preserve at $10-24 flat per scene; and whole-project bands of $2,000-5,000 for a damaged 16mm short
rising to $200,000+ for epic-scale 4K restorations **[TRADE, calibration only]**.

**The finding that matters most:** there is **no market-clearing per-minute rate for AI
colorization.** What exists is DIY SaaS compute at roughly $0.50-0.60 per finished minute, Fiverr
gigs at $5-30 flat, and Upwork color grading at $10-60/hr with **no film-colorization category at
all**. A solo operator selling this is **pricing into a vacuum, not undercutting an incumbent**, 
a rare and genuinely valuable position. **Price against the $195/hr CinePost anchor and the
$2,000-5,000 short-film band, not against Fiverr.**

**Grants: you cannot apply, but you can be the vendor.** NFPF Basic Preservation Grants
($1,000-20,000), state humanities council mini-grants ($500-2,500), NEH Preservation Assistance
Grants (up to $10,000-15,000), IMLS Museums Empowered ($5,000-350,000), **every one requires the
grantee to be a nonprofit or public institution.** An individual cannot apply directly but *can*
be the paid vendor named in someone else's application. That converts an ineligibility into a
sales channel: the pitch is not "hire me," it is "here is a quote and a scope you can paste into
your NFPF application."

Target list, in intent order: local historical and genealogical societies (they hold physical film
and they are the grant-eligible entities), small museums and municipal archives, estate/probate
adjacency, and Facebook local-history groups where the 8mm-in-the-attic conversations actually
happen. **[GAP]**, there is no published data on lead volume or time-to-first-client, and this
document will not invent one.

**Do not compete on scanning.** It is commoditized and thin-margin against a $10k+ Lasergraphics
capital requirement. Partner or outsource the scan; sell the restoration and colorization as the
premium layer.

**The structural insight: the channel is the marketing asset for this stream.** Every published
before/after is a portfolio piece, and portfolio pieces close service deals at zero subscribers.
The first video can generate a commission before it generates a dollar of ad revenue.

### 5.3 The copyright question, two questions, opposite answers

> **This is not legal advice.** The AI-authorship question is actively unsettled: no court has
> ruled on AI colorization specifically, and the Copyright Office has said its line-drawing is
> case-by-case. Get a licensed IP attorney before building a business on any of it.

Two questions get conflated constantly and they have different answers.

**Q1, May you legally sell prints of a colorized frame from a PD film? Almost certainly YES.**
If the underlying film is genuinely public domain, nobody holds rights your derivative could
infringe. Three caveats bite in practice: verify PD status per film rigorously (archive.org labels
are frequently wrong; watch for a still-copyrighted score, a URAA-restored foreign work under
17 U.S.C. §104A, or a copyrighted re-edit); a third party's *scan* may carry contractual claims
even though, per *Bridgeman Art Library v. Corel Corp.*, 36 F. Supp. 2d 191 (S.D.N.Y. 1999), a
slavish reproduction of a PD work is not independently copyrightable; and **rights of publicity
and trademark are separate from copyright and survive it**, selling a print of an identifiable
person commercially can raise publicity issues in some states regardless of the film's status.

**Q2, Do you own a copyright in the AI-colorized output? Almost certainly NO.** The primary
sources here are unusually direct.

From the **Compendium of U.S. Copyright Office Practices, §909.3(A)**
([copyright.gov](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap900/chap900-draft-3-15-19.pdf))
**[PRIMARY-LEGAL]**:

> "Typical technical alterations that do not warrant registration include aligning pages and
> columns; repairing faded print and visual content; and sharpening and balancing colors, tint,
> tone, and the like, **even though the alterations may be highly skilled and may produce a
> valuable product**."

Skill and commercial value are explicitly irrelevant. The Office's own worked example is nearly
this exact fact pattern: Sarah Smith scans old family photographs and "uses software that
**automatically** smoothens the creases ... The registration specialist will refuse to register
the claim, because the use of automated software to smooth preexisting photographs was
de minimis." Decided against, in the Office's manual, before AI was the issue.

**The 1987 colorization precedent is real, and it does not save you.** Compendium §906.3 cites
*Copyright Registration for Colorized Versions of Black and White Motion Pictures*, 52 Fed. Reg.
23,443 (June 22, 1987), colorized versions of B&W films **are** registrable as derivative works
when a human makes sufficient creative choices. But §906.3 also states that "merely adding or
changing one or relatively few colors ... is not copyrightable, **regardless of whether the
changes are made by hand, computer, or some other process**." And the 1987 decision expressly
preserved the underlying film's PD status "so that others, such as competing colorizers, may
exploit the work." The Office's contrasting worked example is Clara Connor, who colored a PD
photograph "to make it appear as if the photo was taken in a different season", note what she
did: a **deliberate expressive choice**, executed. Running inference to recover *plausible
original* colors is the opposite. It is restoration toward a target, not expression.

**Human authorship is now settled law.** *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, No. 23-5233 (D.C. Cir.,
2025-03-18) held human authorship is required as a matter of statutory law
([opinion PDF](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf))
**[PRIMARY-LEGAL]**; the Supreme Court **denied certiorari on 2026-03-02**, No. 25-449, leaving it
standing ([Mayer Brown alert](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/supreme-court-denies-review-in-ai-authorship-case))
**[TRADE]**.

There is one source that cuts the other way and it is worth naming rather than resolving too
neatly. The Copyright Office's **Part 2: Copyrightability** report (2025-01-29)
([PDF](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf))
**[PRIMARY-LEGAL]** distinguishes assistive from generative use, "**assistive uses that enhance
human expression do not limit copyright protection**", and its footnote 63 lists, among
acceptable assistive uses, the MPA's examples of **"color correction, detail sharpening, or
de-blurring."**

These reconcile. Part 2's carve-out protects works that **already have human authorship**, a film
someone shot, from losing protection because AI touched post. It does not manufacture new
authorship in a public-domain work that contains none of yours. **The restoration is assistive to
nothing of yours.**

| If your pipeline is... | Copyright in the output? |
|---|---|
| Load PD film → run colorization/upscale → export | **No.** De minimis under §909.3(A) |
| The above + manual per-region color decisions you chose and can describe | **Maybe, thin.** The Clara Connor / 1987 path. Possible, not assured |
| A curated compilation, your selection, sequencing, narration, score, arrangement | **Yes, in the compilation.** The *Zarya of the Dawn* model |

**What this means commercially, and it is less bad than it sounds:**

1. **You can sell the prints.** Q1 and Q2 are independent. Owning nothing does not stop you
   selling anything.
2. **You cannot stop anyone copying them.** No copyright means no takedown that survives a
   counter-notice, and no exclusivity.
3. **Compete on what copyright does not grant:** print quality, curation, framing, the story
   attached to the image, and decisively **the audience**. Your moat is the channel, not the file.
4. **Never claim a copyright you do not have.** No "© 2026" on an AI-colorized PD frame, no
   registration describing an automated pipeline as authorship, no takedown notices. Overclaiming
   invites §512(f) exposure and reputational damage in a niche that cares about provenance.
5. **The genuinely defensible asset is the compilation**, the video, the sequencing, the
   narration, the score. That *is* protectable, and it is the thing you were making anyway.

### 5.7 Stock licensing, skip it

Two facts compound: the underlying footage is PD so a buyer can get it free and restore it
themselves, and your output is probably not copyrightable (§5.3), so **you may have nothing to
license.** Stock licensing is the sale of rights; if you hold none, you are selling convenience.

**Pond5 is the only open door.** Its Contributor Agreement (v20250115) explicitly allows PD
material, designate it as PD at upload, **but** archival/vintage material goes through separate
vetting requiring proof of copyright ownership of your portfolio. Payout is **30% non-exclusive /
40% exclusive**; this changed in January 2025, so **the widely-cited 60% figure is stale**. The
irony: Pond5's own Public Domain Project gives PD footage away free and pays contributors nothing.

Everyone else is closed. Adobe Stock states verbatim **"Don't submit what you believe to be
public-domain content."** Shutterstock and Storyblocks require original work and **ban
AI-generated content**. Artgrid is invite-only cinematic B-roll; Filmsupply represents filmmakers'
own projects; Framepool and Historic Films are closed curated archives.

**The AI policy trap:** Pond5, Shutterstock and Storyblocks all ban AI-generated content, and
**none publish language distinguishing "AI-colorized real footage" from "AI-generated" content.**
That is the kind of gray zone that gets resolved against you after the work is done. Clear it with
Pond5's vetting team **in writing before investing effort**, not after.

The one concrete earnings datapoint in this niche is Filippo Carlot at roughly €1,000/month across
four platforms, but note what that is: **personally-acquired 8mm/Super-8 reels he physically
owned**, a materially cleaner legal position than archive.org scans. **[GAP]**, zero reported
earnings were found for verified public-domain footage through any paid channel.

### 5.8 The cross-cutting point

The same fact, that the output is probably not copyrightable, has **opposite consequences** by
stream. It is **fatal to licensing** (which is literally the sale of rights), **nearly irrelevant
to prints** (selling does not require owning), and **completely irrelevant to commissions**
(the client owns their own film; you are selling labour). That is another reason commissions rank
first: it is the only stream the copyright problem cannot touch.

## Part 6: revenue roadmap by stage

### Stage 0, Pre-launch (now, nothing published)

**The top three actions, in order:**

> **1. Decide the format before you publish anything: narrated documentary pieces, not silent
> before/after loops.**
> This is one decision that simultaneously resolves the YPP acceptance risk (§1.2-1.3), clears
> TikTok's 60-second minimum and its "adds new ideas" clause (§4.2), clears Meta's originality
> carve-out (§4.3), and unlocks the 40-300x long-form RPM (§4.5). Everything else in this document
> is downstream of it. Getting it wrong is expensive to reverse, because enforcement is
> channel-level and retroactive, a back catalogue of silent loops is a liability, not an asset.

> **2. Stand up the commissioned-restoration service now, before the channel exists.**
> It is the only stream that pays at zero subscribers (§5.1), the only one the copyright problem
> cannot touch (§5.8), and it is priced into a vacuum rather than against an incumbent. Contact
> three local historical societies with a vendor quote they can paste into an NFPF or state
> humanities application. Price against $195/hr and the $2-5k project band.

> **3. Work backwards from 2027-02-01.**
> YPP entry thresholds double for new applicants on that date, and existing members are
> grandfathered (§2.2). Accounting for a ~1-month review queue, the real deadline is around the
> end of December 2026. Set the publishing cadence to reach 1,000 subs + 4,000 watch hours by
> then, and turn on advanced-features verification today because it is not instant.

Also at this stage, all cheap and one-time: register the Findmypast (AWIN) and Bookshop.org
affiliate accounts; decide the music policy now (self-scored or provably clear PD only, never
library music, which halves Shorts revenue *and* risks the membership-eligibility clause in §5.2);
and build the provenance-record habit from video one (§1.6).

### Stage 1, 0 to 1,000 subscribers

Revenue here is **effectively zero from the channel**, and that is normal. Do not optimize it.

- **500 subs:** switch on channel memberships (expanded tier, §2.1). Expect ~$8/month at realistic
  conversion. Do it anyway, it is free and the 70/30 split is better than ads.
- **1,000 subs + 4,000 hours:** apply to YPP immediately. This is the milestone that matters.
- Keep affiliate links in every description; expect single-digit dollars.
- **The real revenue in this stage is commissions**, and the videos are the portfolio that closes
  them.

### Stage 2, 1,000 to 10,000 subscribers

- **Ads become real but modest.** RPM is set by geography and mid-roll placement more than niche.
- **Memberships/Patreon: $8 → $85/month** at the observed 0.13% conversion rate; up to ~$650 at an
  optimistic 1%.
- **~5,000 subs: begin outbound sponsor email.** MyHeritage first: the product match is exact.
  Price off trailing-90-day median views at a $20-40 CPM proxy, not off subscriber count. At 20k
  median views that is $400-800 an integration, which is more per hour than anything else here.
- **Add prints only once traffic exists**, and use Etsy + Printful, not Redbubble (§5.6).
- Short-form is still marketing, not income.

### Stage 3, 10,000 to 100,000 subscribers

- **Sponsorships become the primary line.** At 50k median views, $1,000-2,000 per integration,
  $1,400-2,800 for a dedicated video.
- **Memberships reach $85 → $850/month** at the pessimistic rate.
- **Commissions scale into institutional work**, museums and municipal archives with budget
  cycles, where being the named vendor in grant applications compounds.
- TikTok becomes worth real effort if the 60s+ format is established: $200-500 per million public
  views, 5-10x Shorts.

### Stage 4, 100,000+

- Memberships alone plausibly $850-6,500/month.
- Sponsorship rates of $4,000-8,000 per integration at 200k median views.
- **Nebula becomes plausible** (§5.4), still curated, still no open application.
- Prints and affiliate remain rounding errors; the ceiling on both is set by traffic, and traffic
  now exists, but neither will ever rival sponsorship revenue per hour of work.

## Part 7: what is genuinely uncertain

Listed so it is not mistaken for settled:

1. **Whether an AI restoration pipeline constitutes "substantive modifications" under the reused
   content policy.** The determinative phrase, applied to a case YouTube has never published
   guidance on. Discretionary and human-judged.
2. **Whether AI colorization of real historical footage requires disclosure.** Upscaling is exempt
   by name; colorization is unaddressed. No source resolves it. (Recommendation: disclose anyway, 
   the cost is zero.)
3. **Whether restoration work attracts its own copyright.** Legally contested and
   jurisdiction-dependent; it drives whether third-party claims on restored PD films are
   legitimate.
4. **Any real precedent for an AI-restoration channel passing or failing YPP review.** No
   first-hand creator account was found either way, only vendor blogs asserting it works, which
   have an interest in the answer. **This is the single most valuable missing datapoint, and the
   cheapest way to get it is to ask in r/PartneredYoutube rather than to search for it.**
5. **Genealogy-company sponsorship spend and rates**, the most on-theme sponsor category,
