# FACTS — Ep01 "A Trip Down Market Street" (Miles Brothers, 1906)

Research date: 2026-09-30. Every fact below was taken from a page fetched in this session.
Cite by ID (F01, F02, ...). Tags:

- **VERIFIED**: two or more independent reputable sources, or one primary/authoritative source
  (Library of Congress, USGS, NPS, archive.org item metadata, FHWA, peer-reviewed article, major press).
- **SINGLE-SOURCE**: one source only, or the sources are not independent of each other.
- **DISPUTED**: the sources disagree. Both readings are given. Do not narrate either one as settled.

**About the quotes.** Quotes marked [exact] were pulled from the raw page text or PDF and are
word for word. Quotes marked [tool] came back through a web-fetch summariser. They should be

## 1. The film, its makers, and how it survived

**F01: Makers.** The film was made by the Miles Brothers, a San Francisco and New York film company. The partners were Harry (the senior partner), Herbert, Earl and Joe Miles. **VERIFIED**
- S1 Kiehn/LOC [exact]: "Harry Miles was the senior partner, with brothers Herbert, Earl and Joe, of the Miles Brothers, a pioneering film production and distribution company, the first with offices on both coasts."
- S2 LOC catalog [exact]: contributor "Miles Brothers".
- Spelling: Kiehn writes "Earl". Atlas Obscura (S14) and PetaPixel (S20) write "Earle". Wikipedia (S5) writes "Earl C. Miles". Use "Earl" to match the primary source.

**F02: Who cranked the camera.** Kiehn names Harry Miles as the man aboard the cable car cranking the camera. CBS reported that descendants describe Earl Miles as "the man who supervised the filming". **DISPUTED** (on emphasis rather than fact; both can be true)
- S1 [exact]: "a Market Street cable car approached the Ferry Building with Harry Miles aboard cranking a movie camera for 12 minutes".
- S4 CBS [exact]: "Miles and his uncle Dwayne are descendants of Earl Miles, the man who supervised the filming."
- Safe narration: "Harry Miles cranked the camera, according to historian David Kiehn."

**F03: Camera setup and route.** The camera was rigged on a United Railroads cable car at 8th Street, near the brothers' studio, and filmed the run east down Market Street to the Ferry Building. It kept filming as the car was turned 180 degrees on the turntable. **VERIFIED**
- S1 [exact]: "The camera was rigged on a cable car at 8th Street, near the Miles Brothers studio. Just after 3 o'clock on that sunny Saturday afternoon, the hand cranked camera recorded the journey to the Ferry Building, the 180-degree rotation on a turntable to face Market Street, and the dramatic ending of newsboys waving at the camera."
- S2 LOC catalog [exact]: "the film begins at the location of the Miles Brothers film studio, 1139 Market Street, between 8th and 9th Streets".
- S4 CBS [exact]: "This is where the original film started, right here about 8th Street," (Rick Laubscher, Market Street Railway).

**F04: Why a cable car.** The brothers first tried shooting from a tripod in an automobile, but the cobblestones blurred the picture. At the end of March 1906 they asked United Railroads for the use of a cable car, and the request was granted. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (Kiehn, but primary)
- S1 [exact]: "They first put a camera on a tripod in an automobile, but the cobblestones on the street created a continuous vibration that blurred the images, so, at the end of March, 1906, they requested the use of a cable car from the United Railroads, owner of the Market Street line, and it was granted."

**F05: Distance and speed.** The filmed ride covers 1.55 miles at an average of nearly 10 mph. Market Street is 120 feet wide. **SINGLE-SOURCE** for distance and speed (LOC note). **VERIFIED** for the 120 ft width (LOC and CBS).
- S2 [exact]: "The filmed ride covers 1.55 miles at an average speed of nearly 10 miles per hour. Market Street, graded through sand dunes in the 1850's, is 120 feet wide".
- S4 [exact]: "Market Street is three miles long, 120 feet wide".

**F06: Length and runtime.** The runtime depends on projection speed, and the sources quote different figures. Quote one with its condition attached. **DISPUTED** (the figures are not interchangeable)
- LOC catalog, S2 [exact]: "Duration: 8:23 minutes at about 15 fps." Medium: "1 film reel of 1 (533 ft) : si., b&w ; 35 mm."
- Kiehn, S1 [exact]: "cranking a movie camera for 12 minutes".
- Wikipedia (S5) and the Atlas Obscura article (S14) say 13 minutes. Market Street Railway (S8) says "12-minute movie".
- 2018 archive.org files, S3 (file metadata): the cropped versions run 716.79 s (about 11:57) at a 24 fps playback rate made by doubling every second frame of a 16 fps scan. The full 4K .mov lists 404.5 s; this looks like a metadata inconsistency, so do not quote it.
- Safe narration: "about twelve minutes as it's usually shown."

**F07: Hand-cranked.** The camera was cranked by hand. **VERIFIED**
- S1 [exact]: "the hand cranked camera recorded the journey".
- S4 CBS [exact] (Laubscher): "Here comes this contraption down the street with these guys hand cranking this camera furiously".

**F08: How it survived.** Kiehn says the film was "safely on a train for the east coast office" when the earthquake hit. CBS says it was shipped to New York by train "just the night before the quake". The LOC catalog summary, quoting IMDb, says the negative went by train on 17 April 1906. **VERIFIED** (that it had left the city by train before the quake). **SINGLE-SOURCE** (the exact departure date, 17 April, which LOC takes from IMDb).
- S1 [exact]: "But "A Trip Down Market Street," the movie that captured this lost moment in time, was safely on a train for the east coast office of the Miles Brothers film company".
- S4 CBS [exact]: "They had shipped it to New York by train just the night before the quake."
- S2 [exact], quoting IMDb: "The negative was taken by train to the Miles New York office on 17 April 1906, narrowly saving it from destruction by one day".
- Caution: none of the fetched sources says the original *negative* still exists today. The surviving copies are described as *prints* (see F37, M03).

**F09: The studio burned.** The Miles Brothers' Market Street studio was destroyed. Herbert Miles said the company lost $80,000 on it, and they never rebuilt it. **VERIFIED** for the destruction (Kiehn and CBS). **SINGLE-SOURCE** for the $80,000 figure.
- S1 [exact]: "Herbert Miles stated they lost $80,000 on their burned out Market Street studio. ... They would never rebuild their studio; instead they operated their business out of a house at 790 Turk Street."
- S4 [exact]: "Among the buildings destroyed by the quake and fire: the offices of the Miles brothers".

**F10: Made for Hale's Tours.** The brothers advertised the film as made for Hale's Tours theaters, which were fitted out like a railroad coach. Demand for San Francisco footage was so high that they built a two-hour lecture program for theaters across the country. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (Kiehn). The LOC summary also mentions Hale's Tours, but via IMDb.
- S1 [exact]: "The Miles Brothers advertised their two films as made especially for the briefly-popular Hale's Tour theaters, which were outfitted like a railroad coach to show scenic films".
- S2 [exact]: "Produced as part of the popular Hale's Tours of the World film series".

**F11: The leftover-film origin.** For the 1905 Nelson–Britt fight, Harry modified cameras to hold 1,000-ft magazines; most cameras then held 200 ft. The fight was scheduled for 45 rounds but ended in 18, so stock was left over. The brothers used the long-magazine cameras for "A Trip Down Mount Tamalpais", then turned to Market Street. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (Kiehn, primary)
- S1 [exact]: "Harry modified three movie cameras to hold 1000-foot film magazines, when most cameras of the day only held 200 feet at a time. ... the fight only lasted 18 rounds, when Nelson scored a knockout, so less than half the film stock was used. What to do with the rest?" Then: "It was so successful they set their sights on San Francisco's Market Street."
- Note: Kiehn's PDF spells it "Tamalpias", a typo in the source.

## 2. Dating the film

**F12: The old date was 1905.** The film was long dated to 1905. CBS says the Library of Congress had dated it to September 1905. **VERIFIED** (that it was believed to be 1905). **DISPUTED** (the month).
- S4 CBS [exact]: "the Library of Congress had dated the film to September 1905."
- S2 LOC note [exact]: "the film could not have been shot in September 1905 as that month was "bone dry.""
- S12 Mauer & Morton, peer-reviewed [tool]: LOC initially dated it "September or October 1905".
- S8 Market Street Railway [tool]: "previously believed to have been filmed in August 1905". This is an outlier.

**F13: Who re-dated it.** David Kiehn, film historian at the Niles Essanay Silent Film Museum in Fremont, California, re-dated the film. The work became public in a San Francisco Chronicle article (30 April 2010) and on CBS *60 Minutes* (broadcast 17 October 2010, correspondent Morley Safer). **VERIFIED**
- S2 LOC note [exact]: "According to an April 30, 2010 article in the San Francisco chronicle and in a 60 minutes television segment broadcast on October 17, 2010, silent film historian David Kiehn determined..."
- S1 author bio [exact]: "He is historian and film programmer at the Niles Essanay Silent Film Museum in Fremont, California".
- S4 CBS: segment by Morley Safer.

**F14: Clue 1, the puddles.** Kiehn noticed water in the street. Newspapers showed no rain in September 1905, but rain fell in spring 1906. **VERIFIED**
- S4 CBS [exact]: ""There's some water between the tracks, there. Reflection," he observed. The film showed puddles from a recent rain. But the San Francisco newspapers from September 1905 showed no rain at all."
- S4 [exact]: ""Starting around mid-March, and going to the end of the month, there was quite a bit of rain.""
- S2 LOC [exact]: "no heavy rain was reported until late March and into early April of 1906."
- Note: the two sources give slightly different rain windows (mid- to late March versus late March to early April). Wikipedia's "early March or late February" (S5) matches neither, so don't use it.

**F15: Clue 2, license plates.** California issued license plates from 1905, so cars in the film can be traced. Car No. 4867 was registered in January 1906 (CBS) or "early 1906" (LOC). Car No. 5057 is the highest number seen, a Knox issued in February 1906 to the Reliance Auto Company. The film therefore dates from after February 1906. **VERIFIED**
- S1 [exact]: "License plates were issued in California beginning in 1905 ... The highest number seen, 5057, was a Knox automobile issued in February 1906 to the Reliance Auto Company on Fulton Street."
- S4 CBS [exact]: "the chauffeur's car, number 4867, was registered in January 1906. Another one, number 5057, registered in February 1906, suggesting the film was made sometime after that."

**F16: Clue 3, the New York Clipper ad.** An ad in the trade paper *New York Clipper* on 28 April 1906 (ten days after the quake) said the film was made "just one week before the complete destruction of every building shown in the picture." **VERIFIED**
- S4 CBS [exact]: "An ad was run on April 28, 1906, ten days after the quake: "We have the only pictures of any value ever made in San Francisco before the frightful catastrophe," ... "This film was made just one week before the complete destruction of every building shown in the picture,""
- S2 LOC [exact]: "an April 28, 1906 advertisement for the film in the theatrical magazine, the New York Clipper".
- See M02. The "every building" part is advertising, not fact.

**F17: The date usually claimed: Saturday, 14 April 1906, just after 3 p.m.** This is Kiehn's date and the LOC catalog's. **DISPUTED.** The independent shadow analysis in F18 gives 24–30 March 1906, and the LOC's own note also says "on or around April 12".
- S1 Kiehn [exact]: "On April 14, 1906, a Market Street cable car approached the Ferry Building..." and "Just after 3 o'clock on that sunny Saturday afternoon".
- S2 LOC notes [exact]: "Location: Market Street, San Francisco; filmed on April 14, 1906." The same record's longer note says [exact] "suggesting that the film was shot on or around April 12, 1906".
- S8 Market Street Railway [tool]: "shot on or about April 14, 1906".
- Gap: none of the fetched sources sets out the specific newspaper evidence that narrows the date to the 14th. Kiehn's essay asserts the date but does not argue it. Safe narration: "Historian David Kiehn dates it to Saturday, April 14th, four days before the earthquake."

**F18: The shadow-dating study.** Engineer Richard Greene measured shadows in the film (including on the Ferry Building and its surviving cornerstone) and used a solar-position calculator. He concluded the film was most likely shot between 24 and 30 March 1906, at about 3:17 p.m. by the Ferry Building clock. It was published in *Photogrammetric Engineering & Remote Sensing* in August 2011. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (trade-press summary). The paper itself was not read.
- S10 Vision Systems Design [tool]: "was most likely filmed from March 24–30, 1906" and "the tower clock of San Francisco's historic Ferry Building shows a time of 3:17 PM Pacific Standard Time (PST)." Published "in Photogrammetric Engineering & Remote Sensing in August 2011."
- Caveat: shadow geometry is symmetric about the solstice, and Kiehn already noted the sun angle is ambiguous between seasons (see the Kiehn summary in the search results; not fetched). Greene's window conflicts with the 14 April date by 2–3 weeks.

**F19: The Ferry Building clock reads 3:17.** **VERIFIED** (LOC scene list plus the Greene summary)
- S2 [exact]: "The Ferry Building clock reads 3:17."

## 4. The earthquake and fire

**F31: Time and date: 5:12 a.m., Wednesday 18 April 1906.** **VERIFIED**
- S15 USGS GIP 31 [tool]: "At 5:12 a.m. on April 18, 1906, the ground under the San Francisco Bay Area shook violently".
- S17 NPS [tool]: "at 5:12 a.m."
- S16 USGS event page [tool]: "At almost precisely 5:12 a.m., local time, a foreshock occurred" and "The great earthquake broke loose some 20 to 25 seconds later".

**F32: Magnitude: 7.8 or 7.9.** USGS's own pages use both values. **DISPUTED**. Narrate it as "magnitude 7.9" or "about 7.8", or say "nearly 8".
- S15 USGS GIP 31 [tool]: "The magnitude 7.8 earthquake".
- S18 USGS Virtual Tour [exact]: "violently shaken by seismic waves produced by the magnitude 7.9 earthquake."

**F33: Duration of shaking.** Sources range from under a minute to about 60 seconds. **VERIFIED** as "under a minute" or "about 45–60 seconds".
- S16 USGS [tool]: "strong shaking which lasted some 45 to 60 seconds".
- S15 USGS GIP 31 [tool]: "for more than 40 seconds". S17 NPS [tool]: "for a less than a minute".

**F34: Rupture length.** The quake ruptured about 296 miles (477 km) of the San Andreas Fault. **VERIFIED**
- S16 [tool]: "Rupturing the northernmost 296 miles (477 kilometers) of the San Andreas fault". S15 [tool]: "nearly 300 miles".

**F35: The fire.** The fire burned for three days and did more damage than the shaking. **VERIFIED**
- S19 USGS casualties [tool]: "The 3-day conflagration following the earthquake caused substantially more damage than did the earthquake".
- S17 NPS [tool]: "burned for three days" and "destroyed over five hundred blocks".
- S15 USGS [tool]: "fires triggered by broken gas mains raged out of control for 3 days".
- S1 Kiehn [exact]: "a raging fire that lasted three days".

**F36: Destruction.** About 4.7 square miles burned and 28,188 buildings were destroyed. **VERIFIED** (USGS with NPS). About 225,000 people were left homeless out of roughly 400,000.
- S19 [tool]: "The area of the burned district covered 4.7 square mile"; buildings "28,188"; homeless "225,000 from a population of about 400,000".
- S15 [tool]: "An area of almost 5 square miles in the heart of the city was destroyed by shaking and fire".
- S17 NPS [tool]: "Over half of the city's 400,000 citizens lost their homes".

**F37: Death toll.** The official count was low: 478 in the 1907 Board of Supervisors count, or 498 for San Francisco per USGS. Modern research, led by city archivist Gladys Hansen, puts it at over 3,000. In January 2005 the Board of Supervisors set aside the 1907 count. **VERIFIED** for "more than 3,000". The official figure is **DISPUTED** (478 or 498 depending on source).
- S19 USGS [tool]: official "498 deaths in San Francisco"; research shows "over 3000 deaths were caused directly or indirectly by the catastrophe".
- S21 NBC News, 2006 [tool]: "The official death count released by the San Francisco Board of Supervisors in 1907 claimed 478 people died" and "On January 25, 2005, San Francisco Supervisors unanimously passed a resolution authored by Dalessandro and Hansen that set aside the 1907 official death count of 478." In 2006 Hansen herself estimated [tool] "closer to 6,000 [dead]; five to six thousand."
- S15 USGS [tool]: "At least 3,000 people were killed".
- S17 NPS [tool]: "the figure probably lies in the several thousands rather than the less than 600".

**F38: Market Street's fate.** The downtown area along the route was "virtually destroyed". Some landmarks seen in the film survived. **VERIFIED** for downtown largely destroyed. For individual buildings, see F39–F41.
- S2 LOC [exact]: "the devastating earthquake and fire of 18 April 1906, which virtually destroyed the entire downtown area."

**F39: Ferry Building.** It survived the quake, and Navy fireboats defended it from the fire. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (SFMTA, a city agency, authoritative for this)
- S22 SFMTA [tool]: "The well constructed structure survived the 1906 quake and was defended from the post-earthquake fires by naval fireboats."

**F40: Call (Spreckels) Building.** It burned and its interior was destroyed, but the steel frame stood. It still stands today, remodeled, as Central Tower. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (Wikipedia)
- S23 [tool]: "The structure was badly burned and damaged by the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, although it did not collapse due to its steel frame". Remodeled 1937 (per the same page).

**F41: Palace Hotel.** It survived the shaking but was consumed by fire that afternoon. Caruso was staying there. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (Wikipedia)
- S24 [tool]: "Although the hotel survived the initial damage from the early morning April 18, 1906, San Francisco earthquake, by late that afternoon it had been consumed by the subsequent fires." Caruso "was staying in the hotel at the time of the quake".

## 5. Traffic

**F42: The "no traffic rules" line is an impression, not a documented legal fact.** On *60 Minutes*, Morley Safer said it "seems" there were no rules. Rick Laubscher of Market Street Railway answered that keeping to the right was effectively optional. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (an interview remark)
- S4 [exact]: ""And it seems, watching the film, that there were absolutely no traffic rules," Safer said ... "It seems like it. I mean, sort of, people, it was optional to stay to the right. But you know, it seemed to be honored in the breach.""
- S1 Kiehn [exact]: "The first thing one notices in the film is the chaotic movement of traffic: drays, automobiles, sightseeing trolleys, horse-drawn wagons and bicycles weave, dodge and barely miss each other with seeming casualness, and pedestrians show no fear."
- No fetched source documents the actual 1906 San Francisco traffic ordinances. FoundSF (S25) places the arrival of lanes, crosswalks and signals in a later era [exact]: "Attempts to regulate and standardize traffic patterns began during this era, with lanes, crosswalks, traffic signals, and parking regulations slowly emerging". That passage describes the 1920s. Safe narration: "no lane markings or traffic lights yet", but see "Could not verify".

**F43: Streetcars, not cars, were the killers.** San Francisco deaths in 1906, from coroner's records: streetcars 61, horse-drawn vehicles 20, motor vehicles 7, trains 7. **SINGLE-SOURCE** (KQED, Dan Brekke, 2018, from coroner's records)
- S26 [tool]: "The real scourge for people venturing through San Francisco's boulevards was the streetcar." Sources: "city coroner's records digitized by FamilySearch.org and 1906 issues of the San Francisco Call".

**F44: How many cars there were.** In 1906 there were 105,900 automobiles registered in the whole US and 11,100 in California. These are federal estimates, and the table warns that early-year records are incomplete. No San Francisco-only figure was found. **VERIFIED** (FHWA, authoritative)
- S27 FHWA Table MV-201 [exact rows]: Summary "1906 105,900 - 2,200 108,100" and California "1906 11,100 - 230 11,330". In 1905: US 77,400, California 7,890.

**F45: Cars were still a novelty.** **SINGLE-SOURCE** (LOC note)
- S2 [exact]: "in 1905 the automobile was still something of a novelty in San Francisco, with horse-drawn buggies, carts, vans, and wagons being the common private and business vehicles."

## 7. Shorts hooks (each points to its sourced fact)

**H1: The traffic jam was a loop.** One car, plate 4867, keeps reappearing; Kiehn counts six laps. Source F24 (state "six by Kiehn's count").

**H2: Puddles solved a 100-year-old mystery.** Water between the rails proved the film was not shot in dry September 1905. Sources F12 and F14.

**H3: Leftover film from a boxing match.** Spare stock and long-magazine cameras from the 1905 Nelson–Britt fight, which ended in round 18 of a scheduled 45, led to this film. Source F11.

**H4: The clock in the last shot reads 3:17.** The Ferry Building tower clock gives the time of day. Sources F19 and F18.

**H5: The film left town and the studio burned.** The film was on a train east when the quake hit, and the brothers' Market Street studio burned. Sources F08 and F09.

**H6: Streetcars, not cars, were what killed people in 1906.** 61 deaths against 7. Source F43.

**H7: They filmed the same trip again in ruins, and the reel turned up at a flea market.** After the quake the Miles Brothers filmed the ruins, including a trip from Fifth Street down to the Ferry Building. A nitrate reel of it, about nine minutes, was bought at San Francisco's Alemany Flea Market and identified with Kiehn's help. It premiered at the Niles museum on 14 April 2018, and the San Francisco Silent Film Festival restored it in May 2018. Kiehn says they shot "almost two hours" of post-quake film. **VERIFIED** (Atlas Obscura with SFSFF)
- S14 Atlas Obscura, 20 April 2018 [exact]: "On April 14, the Edison Theater at the Niles Essanay Silent Film Museum aired the nine-minute earthquake footage" and "“They shot almost two hours of film after the earthquake and very little of it survives. ## 8. Myths and contested claims

**M01: "Filmed in 1905."** This is wrong. Re-dated to spring 1906 (see F12–F17). The LOC catalog now gives 14 April 1906.

**M02: "Every building you see was destroyed a week later."** This comes from the Miles Brothers' own 1906 ad (F16), which was sales copy. The Ferry Building survived the quake and was defended from the fire (F39). The Call Building's steel frame stood and still stands as Central Tower (F40). Wikipedia (S5) calls the ad claim "hyperbolic". Say "most of what you see" or "the downtown was virtually destroyed" (F38).

**M03: "The original negative survives" or "Prelinger holds the negatives."** PetaPixel (S20) says [tool] "The negatives were stored at the Prelinger Archives". The archive's own record says the Prelinger item is a 35mm *print* "thought to be an original 1906 print" (F48). The LOC holds a viewing print and a dupe negative made later (S2 medium: "viewing print ... dupe neg pic"). No fetched source says the 1906 camera negative survives. Say "surviving prints".

**M04: "Shot on a Bell & Howell camera."** PetaPixel (S20) says [tool] the brothers "hand-cranked a Bell & Howell camera". That is impossible for April 1906. Bell & Howell was incorporated 17 February 1907 (S29 Wikipedia [tool]: "incorporated on February 17, 1907"). Its famous 2709 camera was "first produced in 1912" (S30 Science Museum Group [tool]). Kiehn's essay names no camera make. The camera make is unknown.

**M05: "There were only a few thousand cars in the whole country."** CBS (S4) said [exact] "there were only a few thousand of them in the whole country in those days". FHWA estimates 77,400 US automobiles in 1905 and 105,900 in 1906 (F44). Do not repeat the CBS line.

**M06: "Filmed exactly three weeks before" or "filmed March 24–30."** The Wikipedia lead says about three weeks, following Greene's shadow study (F18). Kiehn and the LOC catalog say 14 April. This is a genuine disagreement; say "days before, most likely April 14th, according to Kiehn" (F17).

**M07: "One car circles ten times."** That is the LOC note. Kiehn, the primary researcher, says six (F24).

**M08: "The traffic was completely lawless."** No source fetched documents the law. The quote is Safer's impression (F42).

**M09: "Three surviving copies" or "four prints."** The sources disagree (F47).

**M10: Harry Miles's death.** Kiehn writes that Harry "would die from a seventh-floor fall from an apartment in New York City in 1908" (S1 [exact]). Mauer & Morton (S12 [tool]) say he "committed suicide by jumping from the seventh story". This is **DISPUTED** in framing and sensitive. If you use it, say "died in a fall in New York in 1908".

**M11: "Debuted April 21, 1906."** Only Wikipedia gives this (S5). No primary source was fetched for it; see "Could not verify".
