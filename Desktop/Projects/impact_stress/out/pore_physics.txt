0. What this document is for
The lab needs two numbers for its 800 nm silica at high volume fraction: how long the pore pressure takes
to relax, and how large a pressure Reynolds dilatancy can generate. Both have closed forms. Neither can be
evaluated without inputs the project does not yet measure.
This document works the physics from the two-phase mass balances up, so that every assumption is visible
and every input can be traced to something measurable. It then gives the measurement routes in order of
cost, including one that skips the hardest input entirely.

1. Three different things get called “pore relaxation”
Before deriving anything it is worth separating three times that all have units of seconds and all get called
the pore relaxation time. They differ by up to nine decades for the same material, so picking the wrong one
is not a rounding error.
(i) The acoustic time, 𝜏ac ∼𝐿/𝑐. How long a pressure signal takes to cross the sample at the speed of
sound in the mixture. For a 2.5 mm drop with 𝑐≈1630 m/s this is 1.5 µs. It governs the water-hammer
stage of impact and nothing else here.
(ii) The fluid-compressibility time, 𝜏𝜒= 𝜇𝑓𝜒𝑓𝐿2/𝜅.
How long it takes to drain a pore-pressure
increment when the only thing storing that increment is the pore water squeezing.
This is the rigid-
skeleton limit: rigid grains, rigid network, compressible fluid. It is the time that appears in Jérôme, Van-
denberghe and Forterre’s validity condition for treating the pore fluid as incompressible, and it is what
inertial_dilatant.poroelastic_time_ratio computes.
(iii) The poroelastic consolidation time, 𝜏𝑝= 𝜇𝑓𝐿2/(𝜅𝐾𝑝). How long it takes to drain a pore-pressure
increment when the storage is provided by the particle network deforming. This is the pore relaxation time
in the sense that matters for dilatancy, and it is the one this document is about.
The three differ because they use different storage terms. Water’s compressibility gives an effective stiffness
of 1/𝜒𝑓= 2.2 GPa. The particle network of a colloidal suspension at 𝜙= 0.5 has a bulk modulus of order
102–103 Pa. The ratio is six to seven decades, and it is the whole reason (ii) and (iii) disagree so violently.
Both (ii) and (iii) are correct answers to their own questions. Neither substitutes for the other. §2 derives a
single equation containing both.

3. The drainage length is a modelling choice
𝐿is the distance pore fluid must actually travel to equilibrate. It is not a material property, and quoting 𝜏𝑝
without 𝐿is meaningless. Three lengths matter for a drop impact.
The particle length, 𝐿= 𝑎. Equilibration between neighbouring pores. Note what happens to the formula:
𝜅∝𝑎2, so 𝐿2/𝜅is independent of 𝑎, and

contains no particle size at all. Small particles do not drain more slowly at the pore scale. They drain
more slowly across a fixed geometric length, because it takes more pores to span it. This is the single most
common confusion in this area and it is worth stating in any talk on the subject.
The contact length, 𝐿= 0.49 𝐷0. The contact-patch radius at the force peak, taken from the Wagner
analysis in stress.py, where it is the computed 𝑟𝑐at peak force rather than an assumption. This is the
length that governs whether the spreading lamella can drain, and it is the length implied by Mobaseri et
al.’s Eq. 3.
The drop length, 𝐿= 𝐷0. Global drainage.
For the lab’s silica these give 𝜏𝑝= 0.6 ms, 1430 s, and 5960 s at 𝜙= 0.50. The spread is seven decades, from
a single material, purely through 𝐿.
The right choice depends on the question. For whether frustrated dilatancy can raise the particle pressure
in the spreading film, the contact length is correct: the fluid must be drawn from somewhere in the film to
fill the dilating region, and the film’s own radius bounds that distance. For whether the packing is locally in
mechanical equilibrium at the pore scale, the particle length is correct.

4.4 Why the ceiling matters
Uncapped, (4.3) predicts 6 × 107 Pa of pore suction for 800 nm silica at 𝜙= 0.50. That is 600 atmospheres
of tension in water. The 2026-08-06 brief flagged this as an open problem, proposed a cavitation cap, and
then withdrew the cap for four reasons, the strongest being that once the pore-throat radius is computed
correctly the capillary ceiling never binds and the capped expression loses all particle-size dependence.
(4.5) is a better answer because it is a statement about what the material can do rather than about what the
fluid can survive, and because it keeps a size dependence through 𝐾𝑝. It also has the right structure: it says
the Darcy expression (4.3) describes a transient build-up rate, valid until the packing runs out of dilation, at
which point the pressure plateaus. The physical picture is a spring being wound up, not a pressure vessel
being burst.
It can be checked against data. Jérôme, Vandenberghe and Forterre measured pore suctions of −10 to
−100 kPa in 170 µm glass beads under impact. That is the one system in this comparison where the suction
was measured rather than extrapolated. §6.4 and §9.3 show what (4.5) gives there, and what had to be fixed
to make it agree.

At the low-salt end the double layers of opposing pore walls overlap across the entire throat. Two things

stiffness. Anyone fitting 𝜅across the salt series should expect two effects, not one.

5.3 Three ways to measure 𝜅
Constant-flux filtration (a Darcy cell). Push fluid through a packed bed at a known volumetric flux
and measure the pressure drop; 𝜅= 𝜇𝑓𝑞𝐿bed/Δ𝑝. This is the definitional measurement. For a colloid it is

coeﬀicient from one experiment, and the Buscall–White and Landman–White framework for extracting both
is standard in the literature.
Confined oscillatory compression. Compress a suspension in a rheometer between a plate and a porous
frit, oscillating at frequency 𝜔, and measure the normal force. Below the drainage frequency the response is
drained and returns 𝐾𝑝; above it the response is undrained. The crossover frequency is 1/𝜏𝑝, which makes
this a measurement of both. The practical obstacle is that most rheometers do not have a normal-force
resolution good enough for a network modulus of order 102 Pa.
