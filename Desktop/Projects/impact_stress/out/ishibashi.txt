A B S T R A C T
The manifestation of shear thickening in concentrated suspensions is influenced by interparticle forces. To clarify 
the role of electric double layer (EDL) repulsions, we measured the viscosity of colloidal silica suspensions as a 
function of shear stress under various salt concentrations (0.1–200 mmol/kg) and particle diameters (2a =
100–2500 nm), using a refractive index-matching solvent to minimize van der Waals attractions. Increasing salt 
concentration weakens EDL repulsions, reducing both the shear-thinning slope and the critical shear stress σc at 
the onset of shear thickening. σc becomes nearly constant above a salt concentration which we term the critical 
concentration for critical stress (CCCS). This finding suggests that the effects of EDL repulsions on σc disappear 
above the CCCS, analogous to the critical coagulation concentration (CCC) described by the Derjaguin-Landau- 
Verwey-Overbeek (DLVO) theory. These trends and the viscosity curves were quantitatively reproduced by 
dynamic simulations incorporating hydrodynamic, contact, Brownian, and EDL forces. At a low salt concen­
tration, simulated results reproduced σc∝a−2 observed in the experiments, despite the model using a repulsion 
that is proportional to a. The theoretical explanation for this relationship remains unclear. These results highlight 
the need for further investigation into size-dependent effects of DLVO and non-DLVO forces on rheological 
behavior of concentrated colloidal suspensions.

1. Introduction
Flows of suspensions, mixture of solid particles and liquid, are 
ubiquitous in nature and industry [1,2]. As particle concentration in­
creases, the viscosity η = σ/˙γ of suspension becomes dependent on 
applied shear rate ˙γ or shear stress σ even when the dispersion medium is 
Newtonian fluid. Typically, at low ˙γ, concentrated suspensions often 
show shear thinning, a phenomenon where the viscosity decreases as ˙γ 
increases. Beyond a critical shear rate ˙γc (or critical shear stress σc), 
concentrated suspensions undergo shear thickening, a phenomenon 
where the viscosity rises with increasing ˙γ [3–5]. Shear-thickening sus­
pensions exhibit impact-induced solidification enabling humans to 
“walk on water” [6]. Such dramatic stress-dependent behavior makes 
them applicable for shock absorbers [2]. Under vibration, concentrated 
suspensions show unique dynamic behaviors such as dancing, bouncing, 
and climbing up the wall of container [7,8]. Shear thickening behaviors 
of suspensions intricately depend on various parameters such as particle 
volume fraction, particle size, particle shape, dispersion medium, and 
interparticle interactions [9]. To understand the mechanism of shear 
thickening, studies on simplified model systems—exemplified by sus­
pensions of monodisperse spherical colloidal particles—are required.
For colloidal suspensions, electric double layer (EDL) repulsions and 
van der Waals (vdW) attractions play a role in non-Newtonian behav­
iors. EDL repulsion is due to the overlapping of ion layers formed around 
the charged surfaces of particles. VdW attraction between nonpolar 
material is the consequence of interaction between electric dipoles 
induced by quantum mechanical fluctuations [10]. Aqueous suspensions 
of colloidal particles show high viscosity at low ˙γ when stabilized by EDL 
repulsions. As the shear rate increases, the relative strength of EDL 
repulsion compared to the shear force decreases, which manifests as 
shear thinning. Gradual salt addition weakens EDL repulsions and de­
creases the viscosity at low ˙γ. When salt concentration is raised further, 
the viscosity reaches a minimum value and then significantly increases 
due to vdW attractions [11,12]. The aggregation of colloidal particles 
due to vdW attractions increases the apparent volume fraction and the 
viscosity of suspensions, and shear-induced breaking of particle aggre­
gates results in shear thinning [13]. Aqueous suspensions of montmo­
rillonite at certain particle concentration behave as gel at low and high 
salt concentrations, and as sol at intermediate salt concentrations [14]. 
In contrast, semi-dilute suspensions of fibrous particles have been re­
ported to behave as sol at low and high salt concentrations but as gel at 
intermediate salt concentrations due to the formation of network 
structure [15].
The salt concentration dependences of colloidal suspensions 
described above can be explained by Derjaguin-Landau-Verwey- 
Overbeek (DLVO) theory. DLVO theory is widely accepted in colloidal 
science because the theory can explain the existence of critical salt 
concentration at the transition point from dispersed to coagulated state 
(critical coagulation concentration, CCC or critical coagulation ionic 
strength, CCIS) and the dependence of CCC or CCIS on the ion valence 
(Schulze-Hardy rule) [10,16–18] or surface charge density [19–21]. In 
DLVO theory, the net interaction is described as the sum of EDL repul­
sion and vdW attraction. At low salt concentrations, EDL repulsions 
prevent particle aggregation. As salt concentration increases, EDL re­
pulsions become weaker, and thus the coagulation rate increases. 
Beyond the CCC, EDL repulsions are sufficiently small for vdW attrac­
tions to dominate. The coagulation rate is almost constant independent 
of salt concentration above the CCC [18–21]. The pH adjustment to the 
isoelectric point of the particle surface also weakens EDL repulsions and 
causes aggregation in the absence of other repulsive forces [22,23]. 
Concentrated colloidal suspensions show similar salt concentration and 
pH dependence on the onset of shear thickening. Previous studies have 
observed the decrease in ˙γc (or σc) [11,24–28] and the hysteresis due to 
vdW attractions [29] when EDL repulsions are weakened by the addition 
of salts or pH adjustment.
For non-aggregated suspensions of spherical particles, scaling laws 
for ˙γc or σc as a function of particle radius a have been discussed based on 
a force balance model. The scaling law ˙γc∝a−2 was explained by 
assuming that EDL repulsions balance hydrodynamic lubrication forces 
(∝˙γa3/h, h: the distance between particle surfaces) at the transition point 
from shear thinning to shear thickening [9,30]. In addition to particle 
size, ˙γc also strongly depends on parameters such as particle concen­
tration, viscosity of dispersion medium, and temperature. Meanwhile, σc 
remains almost independent of these parameters, unless interparticle 
interactions change [25–27,31–34]. Thus, it is more convenient to use σc 
to discuss the force balance at the onset of shear thickening. Previous 
studies experimentally confirmed that σc∝a−2 holds under strong EDL 
repulsion, whereas σc∝a−3 is observed in Brownian suspensions where 
EDL repulsions are weakened by the addition of electrolytes [4,5,11,25, 
26,35–37]. Moreover, the relation σc∝a−2 was also observed in the 
suspensions sterically stabilized by polymers on the particle surface [38, 
39]. In a simple explanation, σc is the shear stress at which σa2 ≈F 
where σa2 is shear force and F is the magnitude of the interparticle 
repulsive force. The above explanation rests on the assumption that the 
magnitude of EDL repulsion and steric repulsion is independent of 
particle size, and the Brownian force proportional to a−1 is dominant for 
the weak repulsion cases. However, the expressions of EDL repulsions 
generally used in DLVO theory are proportional to the particle radius a, 
and in that case, the scaling should be σc∝a−1, which is different from 
the experimental results. The reason for this inconsistency remains un­
clear [35–37].
Regarding the mechanism of shear thickening, a hypothesis attrib­
uting it to shear-induced frictional contacts has been proposed [33, 
35–38,40–43]. In highly concentrated suspensions, a jump in viscosity 
with increasing ˙γ, known as discontinuous shear thickening (DST), is 
often observed. However, previous simulation studies without frictional 
contacts failed to reproduce DST behavior. Simulations using discrete 
element method with hydrodynamic lubrication forces (lubrication 
flow-discrete element method, LF-DEM) have shown that incorporating 
frictional contacts can reproduce DST behaviors [40,41]. LF-DEM sim­
ulations demonstrated that the shear-induced contact scenario works 
even in Brownian suspensions, and revealed the following scaling 
equation:

we systematically measured viscosities and zeta potentials under various 
salt concentrations and particle sizes for suspensions of monodispersed 
spherical silica particles dispersed in the refractive index-matching 
solvent. We compare the experimental values of σc with the calculated 
values using LF-DEM simulation with the model of EDL repulsion in 
DLVO theory. Both experimental and simulated results unveil the exis­

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials
Monodispersed spherical silica particles with different diameters, 
Seahostar KE-P10 (2a = 110 nm), KE-P30 (280 nm), KE-P50 (530 nm), 
KE-P100 (1060 nm), and KE-P250 (2580 nm) from Nippon Shokubai, 
were used as solid particles in suspensions. The manufacturer reports 
that the refractive index of KE-P silica is 1.43, and its true mass density is 
1.9 g/mL. The powder of silica particles was heated at 120◦C over 20 h 
to reduce moisture and possible residual lower alcohol remaining from 
the manufacturing process. The polyethylene glycol 200 (PEG200; Wako 
Pure Chemical Industries, JIS Special Grade) solutions with dissolved 
KCl (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, JIS Special Grade), hereafter 
referred to as KCl-PEG200 solutions, were used as the dispersion me­
dium. The density of PEG200 ρPEG is 1.13 g/mL, the refractive index is 
1.46, and the relative permittivity εr is approximately 20 at 25◦C [46]. 
The viscosities of KCl-PEG200 solutions were measured using a 
viscometer (microVISC, RheoSense), and the results are shown in 
Table 1. KCl was added at various concentrations to vary the surface 
potential and the strength of EDL repulsion. Suspensions of silica par­
ticles dispersed in PEG200 were used because the matching of refractive 
indices of silica and PEG can minimize the effects of interparticle vdW 
forces [10]. The suspensions were prepared by mixing the silica powders 
and the dispersion medium with a planetary centrifugal mixer (AR-100, 
Thinky). The particle concentration was 65 wt% in weight fraction, 
which is about 52 vol% in volume fraction.

2.2. Zeta potential measurements
To characterize the charging behavior of silica particles in PEG200, 
zeta potentials of particles were measured as a function of KCl concen­
tration and particle size. Zeta potential is widely used when calculating 
the EDL repulsive force. The zeta potential measurements were per­
formed by colloid vibration current (CVI) method using an electro­
acoustic zeta potential probe (DT-1202, Dispersion Technology) and by 
electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) method using a light scattering 
instrument (Zetasizer Nano, Malvern Panalytical). The zeta potential 
measurements were performed at 20◦C. The zeta potential values were 
averaged over five trials.
In the measurements with CVI method, we used the values of the 
density, weight fraction, and diameters of the silica particles as the input 
to the device, as well as the density, relative permittivity, and viscosity 
of the KCl-PEG200 solution. The values of electrical conductivities were 
also input so that the output values of κa corresponds to the calculated 
values using κ−1 shown in Table 1.
Since ELS method is difficult to apply for concentrated suspensions, 
the suspensions were diluted to 0.1 wt% for KE-P30, 50, 100, 250 and 
1 wt% for KE-P10 suspensions with the corresponding KCl concentra­
tions in PEG200. The measured electrophoretic mobilities were con­
verted to zeta potentials ζ via Henry-Ohshima equation [49].
2.3. Viscosity measurements
The shear rate dependence of steady shear viscosity of suspension 
was measured under various KCl concentrations and particle sizes. The 
measurements were performed on a shear-rate-controlled rheometer 
with concentric double-cylinder geometry (ONRH-1, OhnaTech). The 
internal diameter of concentric double-cylinder was 19.36 mm, and the 
external diameter was 21.00 mm. The viscosity measurements were 
performed at 20◦C. The range of shear rate was 0.01–1000 s−1.
Starting from the lowest shear rate, the steady shear viscosity was 
measured while increasing the shear rate by a factor of 101/5 at each 
step. After the measurements at high shear rate, we measured viscosities 
again starting from the low shear rate side and confirmed the repro­
ducibility. For small particles (KE-P10) and low KCl concentration (0.1, 
1 mmol/kg), even a slight increase in shear rate caused the rapid rise in 
viscosity near ˙γc, so we used finer shear rate steps. For large particles 
(KE-P250) and high KCl concentration (100, 200 mmol/kg), ˙γc was low, 
and the measured viscosity showed noticeable variability at low shear 
rates. Thus, we adjusted the shear rate range to 0.002–200 s−1 and 
averaged the measured values from three trials for those conditions. In 
this study, σc was determined as the shear stress at which the viscosity 
reached its minimum value before shear thickening.

3. Results
3.1. Salt concentration and particle size dependence of zeta potential
Fig. 1 presents the zeta potentials of silica particles dispersed in KCl- 
PEG200 solutions measured by CVI and ELS method. The results of both 
CVI and ELS measurements demonstrated that silica KE-P particles were 
negatively charged in PEG200 at low KCl concentrations, and adding 
KCl decreased the magnitude of zeta potential. Higher salt concentration 
shortens the Debye length κ−1, which lowers the magnitude of zeta 
potential and weakens EDL repulsions. The zeta potentials of silica KE-P 
particles in PEG200 exhibited the KCl concentration dependence, 
whereas almost no correlation with particle radius was found.
The measured values of zeta potential were around the theoretical 
surface potentials calculated by Eq. (7) with σscd = −3 to −1 mC/m2. In 
the ionic strength range of 1–30 mM, the zeta potentials measured by 
CVI tended to show greater absolute values than the theoretical surface 
potentials with σscd = −3 mC/m2, whereas the zeta potentials measured 
by ELS were between σscd = −3 and −1 mC/m2. At an ionic strength of 
0.1 mM, both CVI and ELS measurements yielded slightly greater ab­
solute zeta potential than the absolute surface potential predicted with 
σscd = −1 mC/m2. It should be noted that the surface charge density may 
change depending on salt concentration, as in the case of silica particles 
in water [22,23].
3.2. Salt concentration and particle size dependence of flow curves
Figs. 2 and 3 plot the viscosity η of the suspensions of 65 wt% silica 
and KCl-PEG200 solutions against shear stress σ. We can observe the KCl 
concentration dependence of flow curves from Fig. 2 and the particle 
size dependence from Fig. 3. The suspensions exhibit shear thinning at 
low shear stress and shear thickening at high shear stress. At low shear 
rates, stress induced by Brownian and EDL forces leads to high viscosity. 
A yield stress was observed for the suspensions with 2a = 280 nm and 
530 nm at a KCl concentration of 0.1 mmol/kg due to strong EDL re­
pulsions. Although crystalline structure may be formed at low shear 
stress in such condition, the order-disorder transition does not influence 
the onset of shear thickening [51]. As shear rate increases, the relative 
contributions of Brownian and EDL forces to macroscopic stress decrease 
significantly, resulting in shear thinning, although these forces them­
selves remain essentially independent of shear rate. At the same time, 
hydrodynamic forces increase almost proportionally with shear rate. 
Neighboring particles are pushed closer together by shear-driven forces, 
including hydrodynamic forces and repulsive forces exerted by other 
surrounding particles. Once these shear-driven forces exceed the 
Brownian and EDL forces acting between approaching particle pairs, 
direct frictional contacts are formed, leading to shear thickening [42, 
45].
As a general trend, the viscosity at the lowest shear stress in the shear 
thinning regime is higher for smaller particles (more significant Brow­
nian motion) and lower KCl concentrations (stronger EDL repulsions). 
As particle size and KCl concentration increase, Brownian motion and 
EDL repulsions become less dominant, and thus the shear-thinning slope 
becomes more gradual. Although the pairwise EDL force scales with 
particle radius in Eq. (5), the stress due to EDL repulsions should be 
larger for smaller particles because their typical interparticle gap is 
reduced. For suspensions with 2a = 2580 nm at high KCl concentra­
tions, suspensions exhibited a considerable Newtonian regime at low 
shear stress because the effects of both Brownian and EDL forces were 
minimal.

shear thickening to a lower shear stress. This trend has also been

3.3. Comparison of flow curves between experimental and simulation 
results
LF-DEM calculations confirmed shear thinning and shear thickening 
behavior in the 52 vol% colloidal suspensions, as well as the salt con­
centration and particle size dependences of flow curves, similar to the 
experimental findings (see Figs. S1–4). Fig. 4 compares the experimental 
and simulation results of flow curves at low and high salt concentrations 
(ionic strength I ≈0.1 and 30 mM). The simulation results for 
0.1 mmol/kg salt concentration were very close to the experimental 
results at KCl concentration of 0.1 mmol/kg without fit parameters. A 
higher surface charge density shifts the flow curves to higher shear stress 
in the simulations at low salt concentration. In both experiments and 
simulations, increasing salt concentration reduces the slope of shear 
thinning and shifts the flow curve towards lower shear stress. These 
results demonstrate that the LF-DEM calculation appropriately evaluates 
the effects of EDL repulsions on the flow curve for concentrated colloidal 
suspension and that the viscosity as a function of shear stress is affected 
by the Brownian, EDL, and contact forces. At low shear stress, there is no 
contact, whereas shear-induced contacts appear at high shear stress, as 
shown in the inset pictures of Fig. 4c.
Despite the overall agreement, several discrepancies remain. First, 
for small particles (2a = 100 nm), the simulations did not show the 
significant decrease in σc with increasing salt concentration that was 
observed in the experiments. This discrepancy may be due to limitations

3.4. Salt concentration and particle size dependence of critical shear 
stress
Fig. 5a shows the relationship between σc and KCl concentration for 
the suspensions of 65 wt% silica and KCl-PEG200 solutions. σc is high at 
low KCl concentrations and decreases with the addition of KCl. At KCl 
concentrations higher than a certain value (critical salt concentration for 
critical stress, CCCS), σc becomes nearly independent of salt concen­
tration except for the case 2a = 2580 nm at KCl concentration of 
200 mmol/kg, where shear thinning is too weak to determine σc accu­
rately. At low salt concentration, EDL repulsions are dominant, and the 
onset of shear thickening is determined by the balance between shear 
forces and EDL forces. At salt concentrations above the CCCS, the effects 
of EDL repulsion on σc disappear and the influences of Brownian motion 
or the non-DLVO forces remain. This interpretation is analogous to the 
explanation of salt concentration dependence of aggregation rate in 
colloidal dispersions based on DLVO theory. That is, at salt concentra­
tions higher than the critical coagulation concentration (CCC), the ef­
fects of EDL repulsions disappeared and the remaining vdW force only 
works. The CCCS was observed in various particle sizes in the experi­
ments, whereas LF-DEM calculations showed the CCCS under somewhat 
more limited conditions (see Fig. S8). The discrepancy between the 
experiments and simulations is probably due to the limitation of current 
simulation framework (for small particles at high salt concentration), 
and due to the lack of vdW or non-DLVO forces (for large particles at 
high salt concentration). In the case of 2a = 500 and 1000 nm with σscd 
= −1 mC/m2, the σc values obtained from the averaged flow curves 
agreed well with the experimental results and became nearly constant 
above the CCCS (Fig. 5b), although the values fluctuated in each 
calculation.
Fig. 6a and b compare experimental and LF-DEM calculation results 
of σc as a function of particle radius a at low and high salt concentrations 
(ionic strength I ≈0.1 and 30 mM). At I ≈0.1 mM, both the experi­
mental and calculated values with σscd = −1 mC/m2 scaled approxi­
mately as a−2, whereas the calculated values with σscd = −3 mC/m2 
scaled as a−1. The good agreement between experimental data and 
simulation results with σscd = −1 mC/m2 (Fig. 6a) demonstrates that LF- 
DEM can reproduce the effects of EDL repulsions at a low salt concen­
tration. However, the scaling σc∝a−2 cannot be explained by the simple 
force balance model σca2 ≈F∗because F∗is proportional to a. Although 
the relation σc∝a−1 for σscd = −3 mC/m2 aligns with the scaling pre­
dicted by σca2 ≈F∗, this trend is not observed experimentally. At I 
≈30 mM, the experimental results show σc∝a−2.3, indicating that the 
suspension is not purely Brownian. In contrast, the simulations predict 
nearly σc∝a−3 for small particles, which corresponds to Brownian sus­
pension [4]. This discrepancy also suggests that the simulations may 
overlook non-DLVO forces or overestimate the effects of Brownian 
motion due to the strain-based time step.

4.1. Scaling law for critical shear stress
Fig. 7 shows the relationship between σc and particle radius a, from 
the experimental data for concentrated suspensions of silica and the 
refractive index-matching solvents including the present and previously 
published ones. The solid lines in Fig. 7a and b present σc calculated by 
Eq. (1) with F∗= 0.3 and 0 nN, respectively. For the suspensions of 
65 wt% silica and KCl-PEG200 solution at a KCl concentration of 
0.1 mmol/kg, the experimental results agreed well with Eq. (1) by 
setting F∗= 0.3 nN. In Eq. (5), however, F∗is proportional to a. In the 
conventional theory, it is inappropriate to assume constant F∗for 
different particle sizes. It should be noted that Eq. (1) was derived from 
simulations under a fixed particle diameter without considering the 
particle size dependence of F∗. Interestingly, in the present study, even 
though the EDL repulsion proportional to a was incorporated into the 
calculation, both the experimental values of σc and the calculated values 
with σscd = −1 mC/m2 scaled with a−2 (Fig. 6a). This result differs from 
the prediction σc∝a−1 based on a simple force balance, suggesting that

observed scaling of σc∝a−2 may result from the coupling between EDL 
repulsion and Brownian motion, leading to an intermediate scaling 
behavior between σc∝a−1 and σc∝a−3.
Another possible explanation for the scaling law is the consideration 
of a typical interparticle gap. In the previous discussion, it was assumed 
that the interparticle gap is nearly zero at the onset of shear thickening, 
and the EDL repulsion at that gap was denoted as F∗. This assumption is 
based on the scenario where shear thickening occurs due to interparticle 
contacts. In models that attribute shear thickening to the formation of 
hydro-cluster due to lubrication forces, the interparticle gap at σ = σc is 
considered to remain finite [25]. Even if interparticle contact becomes 
dominant in the shear-thickening regime, it may be reasonable to as­
sume that hydrodynamic lubrication dominates near the σc. Therefore, 
the EDL repulsion used in the force balance model should be evaluated 
at a typical interparticle gap. The typical interparticle gap was calcu­

Solvation force is a short-range repulsive force due to the thin layers 
of solvent molecules formed on the particle surfaces. The hydrogen 
bonding occurs between silanol groups (Si-OH) on the silica particle 
surface and hydroxyl groups (OH) at the end of PEG molecules. As a 
result, the PEG molecules form the solvation layers on the surface of 
silica particles, accompanying the solvation force and the stabilized state 
of silica suspension [54]. Previous studies on aqueous system have re­
ported the reduction of the hydration force between silica in the pres­
ence of salt [55], the change in the frictional coefficient of silica surface 
due to the adsorption of highly or weakly hydrated cations, and the 
hydrated size dependence of frictional force [56,57]. Variation in elec­
trolyte species alters hydrated size or permeability of cations into the 
hydration layer, and these changes are reflected in the macroscopic 
rheological behaviors of silica suspensions, demonstrating the effects of 
hydration forces or the short-range repulsions between the cations just 
outside the hydration layer [28,58]. The hydrogen bonding between 
particles also affects macroscopic rheology, leading to gelation and 
shear jamming [54,59]. If increasing salt concentration reduces the 
solvation forces in our experimental system, the corresponding increase 
in the frictional forces or interparticle hydrogen bonding may raise the 
minimum and the maximum viscosity compared with the low-salt case, 
as shown in Fig. 2. Alternatively, the repulsions between adsorbed cat­
ions may also have an influence on the viscosity. If the solvation forces 
remain at high salt concentrations, the scaling law becomes not purely 
Brownian. For sterically stabilized suspensions, σc∝a−2 holds for the 
hard sphere limit, and σc∝a−1.75 for compressed semidilute polymer 
brush layers [39]. Since the solvation force originates from the layer of 
solvent molecules formed on the particle surface, the scaling law for the 
solvation force is considered to be similar to that for the steric repulsion 
induced by polymers. The present result σc∝a−2.3 at high salt concen­
tration may result from the coupling of solvation, gravitational, and 
Brownian effects, with the solvation contribution making the exponent 
closer to −
2. The hairy layers, known to exhibit non-DLVO effects for 
silica particles in water [22,23], may also contribute to the scaling law of 
σc. In the simulations of this study, those non-DLVO effects and the salt 
concentration dependence of frictional coefficient were not considered. 
Previous studies carried out simulations incorporating the friction that 
inhibits rolling and pointed out that the rolling friction can imitate the 
surface interactions such as hydrogen bonding [43]. Since hydrogen 
bonding can arise from interparticle contacts formed during sedimen­
tation or in the shear-thickening regime, it may particularly affect the 
viscosity and the σc for large particles and high salt concentrations. For 
better understanding the experimental results, improving the friction 
contact model would be an effective approach.
5. Conclusion
In this study, we measured the steady shear viscosity of concentrated 
suspensions of colloidal silica particles dispersed in a refractive index- 
matching solvent as a function of salt concentration and particle size. 
As salt concentration or particle size increased, both the shear-thinning 
slope and the critical shear stress σc decreased. We found that σc became 
constant at high salt concentrations above a critical salt concentration, 
which we refer to as the critical concentration for critical stress (CCCS). 
Above the CCCS, the effects of electric double layer (EDL) repulsions on 
the onset of shear thickening vanished, analogous to the disappearance 
of EDL repulsions above the critical coagulation concentration (CCC) in 
DLVO theory. Further analysis of the CCCS would be beneficial for 
optimizing theory of colloidal interactions in concentrated suspensions 
and for developing methods to estimate interparticle interactions from 
rheological measurements. These experimental trends were quantita­
tively reproduced by LF-DEM simulations for particle diameters 2a of 
500 and 1000 nm, using the DLVO-based model of EDL repulsion with 
an appropriate surface charge density. Simulations reproduced the 
relation σc∝a−2 in the low-salt case, but the theoretical reason behind 
this scaling equation remains unclear. Because the repulsive force is 
proportional to the particle diameter, a model in which shear thickening 
occurs when the shear force balances the EDL repulsion should yield 
σc∝a−1. To address this unresolved issue, two possible explanations can 
be considered at present: one is that the EDL repulsion couples with the 
effect of Brownian motion, and the other is that instead of assuming zero 
interparticle gap, a typical interparticle gap is introduced. In addition, 
the discrepancy between experimental and simulation results at high 
salt concentration suggests the existence of non-DLVO effects. Especially 
for large particles (2a = 2500 nm) and high salt concentration, gravi­
tational force may affect viscosity, as expected from the scaling law of 
σc∝a. Our results indicate the need for further studies to elucidate the 
behavior of DLVO and non-DLVO forces in sheared concentrated sus­
pensions: incorporating non-DLVO forces into simulations and con­
ducting more detailed analysis of the relationship between the size 
dependence of the repulsive force and the scaling law of σc.
