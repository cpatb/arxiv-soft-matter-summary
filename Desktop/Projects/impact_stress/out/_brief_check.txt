The drop never touches the glass cleanly. Air cannot escape the closing gap fast enough and lifts the underside
into a dimple: film thickness ∼𝑅St2/3 ≈340 nm, radius ∼𝑅St1/3 ≈20 µm, gas pressure ∼𝜌𝑈2

figure. The consequence that matters is structural: the gas film screens the water hammer and delays

Stage 1: the water hammer, nanoseconds
At first touch the liquid cannot get out of the way and is compressed. One-dimensional acoustics gives
𝑝= 𝜌𝑐𝑈0 = 5.4 MPa, 700× the dynamic pressure and the largest number in the problem. It is also nearly

• it lasts 𝑡𝑑= 𝑅𝑈0/2𝑐2 = 0.54 ns
• over a contact patch of radius 𝑅𝑈0/𝑐= 1.8 µm
• touching a fraction of order 10−9 of the drop’s volume (the exact figure depends on whether you take
the compressed region as a cylinder or a spherical cap; it lies between 10−12 and 10−9, and is negligible
either way).
Worth saying out loud in a talk: the biggest stress in a drop impact is a billion times too small to matter,
and the air film screens most of what is left.
Stage 2: Wagner, the one that matters
Once the shock detaches the liquid is effectively incompressible and the flow is set by an expanding wetted
disc. Force, pressure field and stress localization all live here. Derived in §2.
Stage 3: the lamella
The drop is now a spreading sheet. Pressure has collapsed; what survives is viscous shear at the wall.
This is the stage that matters for a shear-thickening drop, and it is §5.

The right reason to use the shear stress
Not the one in my first draft. The defensible argument is about total versus effective stress. 𝜌𝑈2
0 is a total
stress applied to a saturated two-phase medium far faster than the pore pressure can equilibrate. Under
undrained loading the pore fluid carries essentially all of an isotropic increment, so the effective particle
pressure, the quantity that actually sets 𝑝= 𝑃/𝑃∗, barely moves. What does generate effective particle
pressure is shear, through Reynolds dilatancy: the packing must expand to shear, the expansion is resisted
by Darcy backflow, and the resulting suction presses the particles together. So the shear stress is a proxy
for a pressure criterion, not a rival to one, and that is what makes it consistent with the ID framework
in §6 rather than in competition with it.

7. Two problems with Eq. 6, and what does and does not fix them
Problem 1: it does not evaluate to 1 at the boundary it is drawn at
With the paper’s own constants at the paper’s own Regime-2/3 conditions (cornstarch, 𝜙= 0.41, 𝑈0 = 5.1

and its shape is right (monotone in 𝜙, rising 1.9× from 𝜙= 0.41 to 0.43). So this is a constant prefactor
of ~150, not a different functional form.

Problem 2: Eq. 3 predicts pore pressures that cannot exist
Look at the last column of §6’s table: 107–108 Pa of Darcy suction for 800 nm particles. A pore fluid cannot
do that.
I proposed capping it at what the fluid can sustain. That fix is now largely withdrawn, and it
is worth seeing why, because three of the four objections are things I would not have caught alone:
1. Wrong capillary length, and it was load-bearing. Air invasion is set by the pore throat, 𝑟throat ≈
0.155𝑅particle, not the particle size. Corrected, the capillary ceiling is 186 kPa for cornstarch and 2.3
MPa for silica, so it never binds and the ceiling is always ~1 atm for both. Then 𝜅, 𝑎, Δ𝜙and 𝐷0
all cancel out of the capped number, and it can no longer distinguish 10 µm cornstarch from 800 nm
silica, which was its entire purpose.
2. The supporting evidence was misread. Jérôme, Vandenberghe & Forterre measured −10 to −100
kPa in 170 µm glass beads, where uncapped Darcy also predicts 104–105 Pa. They demonstrate no
saturation. Their closing paragraph extrapolates to cornstarch and predicts “about 1–10 MPa … in
good agreement with impact stress estimations in cornstarch.” The cited authority asserts the opposite
of what I attributed to it.
3. One atmosphere is not a material constant. Degassed water sustains −25 to −140 MPa. The
1 atm figure is the heterogeneous-nucleation threshold of gas-saturated water, a sample-preparation
property, and therefore a hypothesis you can test by degassing, not a ceiling.
4. Capping a pressure does not cap a force, since 𝑟𝑐keeps growing; and once the fluid does cavitate
the pack granulates and carries a capillary-cohesive stress instead.

Two adversarial referee passes and a code audit ran against the first draft. Here is what they moved, so you
can see which parts have been stress-tested and which have not.
Confirmed exactly, no change: the Wagner pressure identity against Cheng’s Eq. [10]; the wall-shear
