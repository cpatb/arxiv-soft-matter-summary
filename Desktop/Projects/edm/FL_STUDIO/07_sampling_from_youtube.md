# Pulling audio off YouTube for sampling

Two tools, and they do different jobs.

**yt-dlp** talks to YouTube. It works out which audio streams a video has, handles the
signed URLs and the DASH manifest, and downloads the one you ask for.

**ffmpeg** converts. It knows nothing about YouTube and cannot download from it — people
assume it can because it handles every other audio task, but the site negotiation is the
part it does not do. yt-dlp calls ffmpeg for you once the file has landed.

You already have ffmpeg at `C:\Users\compl\ffmpeg\bin\ffmpeg.exe`, and it is on your PATH,
so yt-dlp will find it on its own.

## Install

```powershell
winget install yt-dlp.yt-dlp
```

Then put it on the nightly channel, once:

```powershell
yt-dlp --update-to nightly
```

After that, `yt-dlp -U` keeps you on nightlies. Do this before you troubleshoot anything
else — YouTube breaks yt-dlp faster than the stable channel ships fixes, and a stale
yt-dlp is the cause of almost every "this used to work" failure. As of 2026-08-15 the
stable build was six weeks old and could not download at all.

## The one thing to understand first

**YouTube audio is already lossy.** There is no hidden lossless version. The best you can
get is one of two streams:

| Format code | Codec | Bitrate | Container |
|---|---|---|---|
| 251 | Opus | ~160 kbps | `.webm` |
| 140 | AAC | 128 kbps | `.m4a` |

Both are 48 kHz. Everything below follows from that: your job is to get one of those two
streams onto disk and then *stop degrading it*.

Which means: **never convert a YouTube rip to MP3.** Encoding lossy audio into a second
lossy format stacks a fresh round of artifacts on top of the ones already there, and the
things MP3 throws away — high-frequency transient detail — are exactly the things you
notice once you pitch a sample down or time-stretch it. Decode to WAV instead. WAV is
lossless, so it preserves the source exactly as it is, warts and all, and nothing further
is lost no matter how hard you chop it.

## Your defaults live in a config file

`C:\Users\compl\AppData\Roaming\yt-dlp\config`, written 2026-08-15. yt-dlp reads it on
every run, so these two apply whether or not you type them:

```
-P "C:/Users/compl/Desktop/Music"
--no-playlist
```

Everything lands in `Desktop\Music` and Mixes can no longer run away with you. Override
either one on the command line when you need to — `-P "D:\somewhere"`, or `--yes-playlist`
for a playlist you actually want. Delete the file to go back to plain behaviour.

The forward slashes in that path are deliberate: the config is parsed shell-style, where
a backslash is an escape character.

## Why `--no-playlist` is in there

Copying a URL out of the address bar while a video is playing usually gets you something
like this:

```
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a0jOzp3-upA&list=RDa0jOzp3-upA&start_radio=1
```

The `&list=RD...` is a YouTube **Mix** — an autogenerated radio station, not a playlist you
made. yt-dlp reads `list=` as an instruction and downloads the whole thing. On 2026-08-15
that was 458 tracks landing in `C:\Users\compl`, and it got seventeen deep before it was
noticed, because every single item downloads successfully. There is no error to catch it on.

`--no-playlist` makes yt-dlp ignore `list=` and take only the one video. It is in your
config file now, so it applies automatically; it is repeated in the recipes below only so
they still work if you copy one onto another machine.

The tell, if it does happen, is in the first three lines:

```
[youtube:tab] Downloading playlist RDa0jOzp3-upA - add --no-playlist to download just the video
[download] Downloading playlist: Mix - ...
[youtube:tab] Playlist ...: Downloading 458 items of 458
```

`[youtube:tab]` instead of `[youtube]` means it is in playlist mode. Ctrl+C.

When you *do* want a real playlist, pass `--yes-playlist` and give it its own folder with
`-P "C:\Users\compl\Desktop\Music\SomeSet"`, so a few hundred files do not land loose among
your singles.

## Recipe 1 — for sampling in FL Studio

```powershell
yt-dlp --no-playlist -f bestaudio -x --audio-format wav -o "%(title)s.%(ext)s" "URL"
```

This is what you want almost every time. WAV drops straight into the Playlist, Slicex and
Fruity Granulizer with no codec weirdness. FL handles Opus badly, so do not hand it a
`.webm`.

Set your FL project to **48 kHz** in Options > Audio Settings before you load these.
YouTube audio is 48 kHz; if your project runs at 44.1 kHz, FL resamples every sample on
load. It is a small quality cost, but it is a free one to avoid.

## Recipe 2 — for listening on your phone

```powershell
yt-dlp --no-playlist -f bestaudio -x --audio-format best --embed-thumbnail --embed-metadata -o "%(title)s.%(ext)s" "URL"
```

`--audio-format best` means "keep whatever codec YouTube gave you" — it extracts the audio
stream but does not re-encode it. You get a `.opus` or `.m4a` of a few MB with the title
and cover art attached. Your Moto G plays both.

Do not put WAV on your phone. It runs about 11 MB per minute, so a six-minute track is
65 MB, and WAV carries no tags worth the name, so it shows up in your music app as an
untitled blob.

For maximum compatibility — car stereos, someone else's iPhone, an old Bluetooth speaker —
force the AAC stream, which is also a straight remux with no re-encode:

```powershell
yt-dlp --no-playlist -f 140 --embed-thumbnail --embed-metadata -o "%(title)s.%(ext)s" "URL"
```

**Use a separate download for each purpose.** Do not rip a WAV and then shrink it for the
phone — that shrink is a re-encode, which is the loss you avoided in the first place.
Downloading twice costs you nothing.

## Flags worth knowing

```powershell
yt-dlp -F "URL"                                    # list every available stream first
yt-dlp -f bestaudio -x --audio-format flac "URL"   # same fidelity as WAV, about half the size
yt-dlp -f bestaudio "URL"                          # raw stream, zero processing
yt-dlp --download-sections "*1:23-2:05" -f bestaudio -x --audio-format wav "URL"   # one section only
yt-dlp -a urls.txt -f bestaudio -x --audio-format wav                              # batch, one URL per line
yt-dlp --ffmpeg-location "C:\Users\compl\ffmpeg\bin" ...                           # if it ever cannot find ffmpeg
```

FLAC is worth considering for a sample library you intend to keep. It is bit-identical to
the WAV and halves your disk use, and FL Studio reads it natively.

For a long DJ set or a mix you want to chop, do not bother trimming with
`--download-sections` — pull the whole thing as WAV and let Slicex's beat detection find
the hits. That is covered in `02_first_beat.md`.

## When a download fails

Do these in order. Most failures die at step 1, and the two failure modes below look
nothing alike once you know which line to read.

### Step 1 — update, always, before anything else

```powershell
yt-dlp --update-to nightly
```

Then retry the exact command that failed. It is one command, it takes ten seconds, and it
rules out the most likely cause outright. Do not start changing flags, adding cookies or
reading forum threads until you have done this — you will otherwise spend an hour fixing
something that was never the problem.

Why it dominates: YouTube now requires a **PO token** on almost every player client. yt-dlp
silently drops the formats it cannot get a token for, and the one client that still works
without one keeps changing. When YouTube retires that client, every yt-dlp older than the
change breaks at once, and the stable channel can be weeks behind.

### Step 2 — read *which* error you got

The two failures have different causes and different fixes. Tell them apart by the last
error line.

**`ERROR: unable to download video data: HTTP Error 403: Forbidden`**

yt-dlp found the stream, asked for it, and YouTube refused. This is the stale-client
failure. Cookies will not help — on 2026-08-15 it extracted 566 Firefox cookies and got
the identical 403. Step 1 is the fix. If you want to confirm the diagnosis before
updating, force a different client:

```powershell
yt-dlp --extractor-args "youtube:player_client=web" -f bestaudio "URL"
```

If that switches the message to *"Requested format is not available"*, PO tokens are the
cause — every other client had its formats filtered out, and the last working one has
stopped working. Update.

**`ERROR: Requested format is not available`** *with a 429 further up*

```
WARNING: Unable to download webpage: HTTP Error 429: Too Many Requests
WARNING: Unable to fetch GVS PO Token for web_safari client: Missing required Visitor Data
ERROR:   Requested format is not available
```

Different chain: YouTube rate-limits the initial page fetch → yt-dlp gets no visitor data →
it cannot mint a PO token → the adaptive streams (251, 140) are never offered → `bestaudio`
matches nothing in what is left. Left alone the block escalates and the message becomes the
blunter *"Sign in to confirm you're not a bot."*

Changing the format selector does not help, because extraction fails before selection ever
happens. Go to step 3.

### Step 3 — add cookies, for the 429 / "not a bot" case only

```powershell
yt-dlp --cookies-from-browser firefox -f bestaudio -x --audio-format best "URL"
```

**Chrome will not work for this on Windows.** Since Chrome 127 the cookie database uses
App-Bound Encryption and yt-dlp fails with `Failed to decrypt with DPAPI` (yt-dlp issue
10927). Edge fails the same way, being the same engine. Firefox does not have the problem,
which is why Firefox is installed on this machine — it exists purely to hold cookies for
yt-dlp, and you never need to browse in it.

You do **not** need to sign into a Google account. Having visited youtube.com in Firefox
once is enough. Staying signed out is the better default, since yt-dlp traffic against a
logged-in account is what gets accounts flagged.

Carrying `--cookies-from-browser firefox` on every command is harmless and occasionally
saves you, so keep it in your default command if you like. Just do not read a failure *with*
it as evidence that cookies were the problem.

Treat the cookies as a password: don't commit them or paste them anywhere.

The 429 is per-IP and decays on its own, so waiting a few hours and retrying with no cookies
is also legitimate. Retrying in a tight loop is not — it extends the block.

### Not errors, ignore them

```
[info] Video Thumbnail 41 does not exist
[info] Video Thumbnail 40 does not exist
```

`--embed-thumbnail` walks down the thumbnail list until it finds one that exists. Six or
seven misses before a hit is normal. The line that matters is the one starting `ERROR:`.

## Can you make a rip sound better?

Partly, but not the way people usually try. Lossy encoding is not reversible — Opus decides
what you will not miss and discards it, and nothing deterministic puts it back. Any tool
advertising "restore lost detail" is doing one of two things: inventing plausible content,
or changing the tone in a way you might happen to prefer. Neither is recovery.

More useful: **check whether the codec is even the limiting factor.** Usually it is not.

### What the Ron Hardy Music Box rip actually measures

Welch PSD over a 120 s excerpt from the middle of the set (`probe_spectrum.py`, kept with
the session scratch files). Levels are relative to the 1 kHz level:

| Frequency | Level | |
|---|---|---|
| 5 kHz | −6 dB | still full |
| 10 kHz | −22 dB | rolling off |
| 12 kHz | −30 dB | going fast |
| 14 kHz | −48 dB | gone |
| 16–18 kHz | ≈ −47 dB | flat — this is hiss, not music |
| 21 kHz | −68 dB | Opus cutoff, 68 dB down, irrelevant |

99.9% of the energy sits below **10.1 kHz**, and the practical content edge is ~13 kHz.

The shape is the whole answer. There is no brickwall at 20 kHz, which is what codec damage
looks like — Opus is passing everything faithfully and running out of material long before
it runs out of bandwidth. The ~13 kHz ceiling is a 1986 cassette recording of a PA in a
room. That limit was baked in forty years ago and no processing recovers it.

Two other measurements worth having:

- **L/R correlation +0.945, side/mid −15.3 dB.** The stereo is nearly fake — it is a
  mono-ish source with a little room difference.
- **Dynamic range ~9.5 dB** (median frame vs. 5th-percentile frame), peaks pinned at
  0 dBFS. Already squashed. Do not compress it further.

### So what actually helps

The wins are in removing what should not be there, not restoring what never was.

1. **Low-pass around 14 kHz.** Everything above it is hiss — the flat −47 dB shelf. You
   lose no music (99.99% of energy is below 12 kHz) and the result *sounds* brighter,
   because the veil of broadband noise is what was dulling it.
2. **Sum to mono.** At 0.945 correlation the stereo is costing you a ~3 dB SNR gain and
   giving nothing back, and it avoids phasey artifacts when you pitch the sample down. It
   also halves the file, which is the one thing here that genuinely shrinks it.
3. **Broadband denoise, gently.** Spectral subtraction with a learned hiss profile —
   ffmpeg's `afftdn`, Audacity's Noise Reduction, iZotope RX if you have it. Push it too
   far and you get "musical noise," a watery warbling that is worse than the hiss. Little
   and often.
4. **Transient shaping.** This is the one that actually delivers "crisp." Perceived crispness
   lives in attack transients and the 2–6 kHz band, not at 15 kHz. A transient shaper on
   a drum hit does more than any amount of high-shelf.
5. **Harmonic exciter, if you want it.** Synthesises content above the rolloff. It is
   fabrication, not recovery — but for sampling, fabrication you like is a legitimate
   creative choice. Just be clear with yourself about which one you are doing.

A starting chain, to be A/B'd by ear rather than trusted:

```powershell
ffmpeg -i "in.opus" -af "highpass=f=30,lowpass=f=14000,afftdn=nr=10:nf=-45,pan=mono|c0=0.5*c0+0.5*c1" -ar 48000 -c:a pcm_s16le "out_clean.wav"
```

Always keep the unprocessed WAV alongside it. Denoising is destructive and you will
sometimes prefer the dirty one.

### The thing worth saying last

House was built on exactly this material — second-hand records, cassette dubs, radio rips.
Ron Hardy made his own edits on tape. The band-limiting and the hiss are a large part of why
a sample sounds like a *sample* and sits in a mix instead of fighting it. Clean it up too
thoroughly and you often get something that sounds thinner and harder to place. Do the
low-pass and the mono sum, which are free. Be sparing with the rest.

## Two practical notes

**Quality ceiling.** A rip can never sound better than the YouTube stream, and the YouTube
stream is itself an encode of whatever the uploader supplied. A vinyl rip someone recorded
on a phone is not going to improve because you saved it as WAV. If a track matters to you,
check Bandcamp or Discogs first — the Chicago catalogue is unusually well served on both.

**Clearance.** Ripping for practice, study and your own listening is normal and nobody
cares. Releasing a track built on an uncleared sample is a different thing entirely, and
the house catalogue is actively administered — Trax and Dance Mania material has real
owners who do pursue it. Sample freely while you are learning; clear it before anything
goes out with your name on it.
