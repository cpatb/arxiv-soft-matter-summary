# Arrangement: turning a loop into a track

This is where beginners get stuck, permanently. Making a good eight-bar loop is easy and
satisfying. Turning it into six minutes that hold attention is a different skill, and almost
nobody teaches it because it is less fun to talk about than synths.

## The power of two

Dance music counts in bars, and bars are grouped into phrases of 4, 8, 16 and 32. Changes
happen on phrase boundaries. Not near them. On them.

At 124 BPM:

| Length | Duration |
|---|---|
| 1 bar | 1.9 s |
| 4 bars | 7.7 s |
| 8 bars | 15.5 s |
| 16 bars | 31.0 s |
| 32 bars | 62.0 s |

Eight bars is the basic unit of change. Sixteen is the basic unit of a section. Thirty-two
is a full section in a longer track.

This is not a stylistic preference, it is a functional requirement. DJs mix in and out on
phrase boundaries, and a track whose sections start on bar 13 will not line up with the
record playing against it. Breaking the rule is possible and occasionally great, but you
should be able to hear the rule before you break it.

> **In practice.** If your arrangement feels like it is wandering, count the bars between
> changes. It will almost always turn out that something is happening on bar 7 or bar 21.

## The shape of a house track

The standard form, and a concrete map you can copy directly into FL Studio's playlist. This
is 176 bars, which at 124 BPM is 5 minutes 41 seconds.

| Bars | Section | Time | What is playing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 16 | Intro | 0:00 | Kick, hats, maybe one percussion element. Nothing else |
| 17 to 32 | Intro 2 | 0:31 | Add bass, clap, more percussion |
| 33 to 64 | Main 1 | 1:02 | Everything. Chords, vocal, the full groove |
| 65 to 80 | Breakdown | 2:04 | Drums out. Chords and atmosphere only |
| 81 to 96 | Build | 2:35 | Tension. Riser, snare roll, filter opening |
| 97 to 128 | Main 2 | 3:06 | The biggest section. Full arrangement plus whatever you held back |
| 129 to 144 | Main 2 variation | 4:08 | Change one thing. Drop an element, add a counter-melody |
| 145 to 160 | Outro | 4:39 | Strip back. Chords and bass out |
| 161 to 176 | Outro 2 | 5:10 | Drums only, then stop |

Three things about this that are easy to miss.

**The first 30 seconds are almost empty and that is correct.** It feels wrong when you are
making it, because you are listening to it in isolation and it is boring. It is not going to
be heard in isolation. It is going to be heard underneath the last minute of the previous
record. That empty intro is a mixing handle, and it is the difference between a track DJs
play and a track DJs skip.

**The outro mirrors the intro.** Same reason.

**The energy is not a straight line.** It climbs, drops out completely at the breakdown,
climbs harder, and peaks in Main 2. The breakdown is not a rest, it is a setup. The drop
hits hard *because* you took everything away first, and for no other reason.

## The mechanics of a build

The build is the 16 bars before the biggest moment in your track, and it is the most
technical part of arranging. You are manufacturing tension, which means increasing several
parameters simultaneously and then releasing all of them at once.

Tools, roughly in order of how often they are used:

**Filter automation.** Open a lowpass filter gradually across the build so the whole track
brightens. Or close a highpass so the low end returns. Both work.

**Risers.** A noise sweep or a rising synth tone. FL ships a folder of them under Packs.
They are a cliché and they are a cliché because they work.

**Snare or hat rolls.** Start with hits every 1/4, then 1/8, then 1/16, then 1/32 over the
last two bars. Increasing subdivision rate is read by the brain as accelerating even though
the tempo is constant.

**Rising pitch.** Automate the pitch of a percussion element or a riser upward.

**Volume and reverb increase.** More reverb makes things feel bigger and less defined, which
is useful right before you want dry clarity.

**The silence before the drop.** Cut everything for the last half bar or one beat. This is
the single most effective trick in the entire genre and it costs nothing.

Then on the downbeat of the drop: everything returns, all filters open, and a crash cymbal
marks the boundary.

Two failure modes. If your build has all these things at maximum for all 16 bars, there is
nowhere left to go. Start low. And if you build for 16 bars and then the drop is the same
arrangement that was playing before the breakdown, it will feel like a let-down. Hold
something back: a percussion layer, a counter-melody, an octave of the bass.

## Variation: the actual skill

The reason a good producer's loop holds for 32 bars and yours holds for 8 is not better
sounds. It is that something is always changing.

Practical techniques, cheapest first:

**Mute and unmute.** Drop the hats for two bars. Drop the bass for one bar before a change.
Remove the kick for four bars in the middle of a section. This is free and it is what
professionals do most.

**Fills.** The last bar of every 8-bar phrase should not be identical to the other seven. A
tom fill, a reversed cymbal, a stutter on the vocal, a single extra kick.

**Automation.** Slowly move a filter cutoff, a reverb amount, a delay feedback across 16
bars. The listener will not identify what is changing but will feel that it is alive.

**Layer entries.** Introduce elements one at a time on phrase boundaries rather than all at
once.

**Two-bar and four-bar variants.** Write your hat pattern as a four-bar loop with a
difference in the fourth bar rather than a one-bar loop repeated.

The reliable rule: **something should change every 8 bars, and something big should change
every 16.**

## Transitions

Moving between sections needs a marker or it will feel like a bad edit.

A crash cymbal on the downbeat of the new section. A reverse cymbal leading into it. A short
silence. A drum fill in the last bar. A quick filter sweep. A vocal one-shot. Any of these,
and usually two of them together.

An **impact**, a low booming hit, on the downbeat of a drop is another standard tool and it
is in FL's SFX pack.

## Length, and why

Club tracks are 5 to 7 minutes. Radio and streaming edits are 3 to 4. Make the long version
first. Cutting a long arrangement down is easy; extending a short one is not.

If you are only making tracks for yourself and for practice, 4 to 5 minutes is plenty and
you will finish more of them.

## How to arrange without going insane

Two approaches, and both work.

**Top down.** Build one perfect 8-bar loop with every element you want in the track. Then
copy it across the whole timeline and start deleting things to create the intro, the
breakdown and the build. Subtraction is much faster than addition, and it guarantees the
sections are consistent.

This is the one to use as a beginner. It converts arranging from a creative problem into an
editing problem.

**Bottom up.** Write the track linearly from bar 1. More natural, more likely to produce
something that develops in an interesting way, and much more likely to stall at bar 40.

## The finishing problem

You will make dozens of loops and finish none of them. Everyone does this. It has a name in
producer forums, "loop syndrome", and the cure is procedural rather than creative:

Give yourself a deadline per track. One week, then it is done, then it goes in the
"finished" folder whether or not you like it.

Arrange before you mix. If you polish an eight-bar loop for six hours you will be sick of it
before you ever arrange it.

Accept that the first 30 tracks are practice. They are not going to be good and they are not
supposed to be. See `PRACTICE/8_week_plan.md`.
