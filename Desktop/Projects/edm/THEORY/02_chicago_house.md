# Chicago house

You live about fifteen minutes from where this happened. The building is still standing.

## The Warehouse, 206 South Jefferson Street

In 1977 Robert Williams and Siegfried Schuh opened a members-only club in a three-storey
building in the West Loop, in what was then a warehouse district with nothing else around
it. They brought in a DJ from New York named Frankie Knuckles, who had been playing at the
Continental Baths alongside Larry Levan.

The crowd was mostly Black, mostly gay, and mostly there for a very long night. The club ran
from Saturday midnight into Sunday afternoon. There was no alcohol licence. People brought
their own or drank juice, and the whole thing was oriented around dancing for ten or twelve
hours rather than around a bar.

Knuckles was resident from 1977 to 1982. Disco was dying commercially, the records were
drying up, and he started extending and rebuilding the ones he had: looping breaks on reel
to reel, layering a drum machine underneath a disco record to make it harder and longer,
editing tracks to give himself more of the parts that worked. The music he played did not
have a name, so people asked record shops for the stuff they heard at the Warehouse. The
Warehouse. House.

That etymology is the standard account and it is probably right, though as with all
etymologies there are people who will argue. The city landmarked the building in 2024.

## The Music Box, and the other temperament

Knuckles left the Warehouse in 1982 over a price increase and opened the Power Plant.
Robert Williams reopened the old room as the Music Box and installed Ron Hardy.

If Knuckles was the architect, Hardy was the demolition crew. He played louder, faster,
rougher, and considerably more chaotically. He would drop a record, run it backwards, play
the same track three times in a night, cut the sound entirely. He was an addict and he died
in 1992 at 33, and the recordings of his sets that circulate are genuinely wilder than
almost anything DJs do now.

The two clubs are the two poles of house that still exist today. Smooth, soulful, musical,
sophisticated on one side. Raw, mechanical, confrontational on the other. Deep house and
acid house are the direct descendants.

## The records start

Up to about 1983 house was a way of playing other people's records. Then it became a way of
making your own, because the equipment got cheap.

The specific reason is worth knowing: the Roland TB-303 and TR-808 had been commercial
failures. The 303 was sold as a bass accompaniment for guitarists practising alone and it
sounded nothing like a bass guitar, so it flooded the used market. Kids in Chicago bought
them for very little money and used them wrong.

**Jesse Saunders, "On and On" (1984, Jes Say Records)**, written with Vince Lawrence, is
usually cited as the first house record pressed to vinyl. It exists because a bootleg
medley Saunders had been playing got stolen, so he rebuilt it himself with a drum machine
and a bassline.

**Trax Records** (Larry Sherman, 1984) and **DJ International** (Rocky Jones, 1985) became
the two labels that carried the sound out of the city. Trax pressed on recycled vinyl and
the records sound rough because they literally are, and there is a long and ugly story
about royalties that the artists are still fighting over. Both things are true: the label
distributed the music worldwide, and it paid the people who made it almost nothing.

## The founding records

**Marshall Jefferson, "Move Your Body" (1986, Trax).** Subtitled "The House Music Anthem",
which is the sort of thing you can only get away with if you are right. Jefferson bought a
piano on credit and worked out the riff himself. It put acoustic-sounding piano into a
drum-machine record and invented an entire subgenre in the process.

**Mr. Fingers, "Can You Feel It" (1986).** Larry Heard, a drummer by training, made this
with a Roland Juno-60 and a TR-909 and essentially nothing else. Sparse bassline, ethereal
pads, no vocal on the original. This is where deep house begins, and it still sounds like
the future rather than the past.

**Phuture, "Acid Tracks" (1987, Trax).** DJ Pierre, Spanky and Herb Jackson bought a
second-hand TB-303, could not work out how to program a normal bassline on it, and twisted
the filter knobs while a pattern looped. The result is the squelching, liquid, screaming
sound that defines acid house. Ron Hardy played it at the Music Box for months before it
was released, from a cassette, and the crowd called it "Ron Hardy's Acid Track". Marshall
Jefferson produced the release.

**Steve "Silk" Hurley, "Jack Your Body" (1986).** Went to number one in the UK in January
1987, which was the moment Britain worked out what was happening in Chicago.

**Farley "Jackmaster" Funk, "Love Can't Turn Around" (1986)**, with Darryl Pandy's enormous
gospel vocal on top, is the other side: house as soul music with a drum machine.

**Lil Louis, "French Kiss" (1989).** Famous for the long tempo breakdown where the whole
record slows to a crawl and then rebuilds. Also famous for the moaning, which got it banned
in several places and sold it everywhere else.

## Jack

"Jack" is the Chicago verb. To jack is to dance to house in the specific loose-limbed way
the music asks for, and a "jack track" is a stripped, hypnotic, mostly drums-and-bass record
built for exactly that. Chip E's "Time to Jack", the whole early Trax catalogue.

The word matters because it names the thing that separates Chicago house from the
prettier European house that followed. The point is the body, not the melody.

## What came after, still in Chicago

**Deep house.** Larry Heard, and later Ron Trent and Chez Damier's Prescription label,
Glenn Underground, Gemini. Jazz chords, seventh and ninth voicings, long evolving pads, a
tempo that sits back rather than pushes. This is the most harmonically interesting corner
of the genre and the best place to steal chords from.

**Ghetto house.** Late 1980s onward, centred on Dance Mania records. DJ Funk, DJ Deeon,
Paul Johnson, Jammin Gerald. Faster, dirtier, extremely minimal, built on a 909 and a
sampler and a lot of obscene chanting. Almost no chords at all. It is the punk of house.

**Juke, then footwork.** Ghetto house sped up to around 160 BPM, then broken apart. Triplet
kick patterns, chopped vocal samples repeated to the point of abstraction, huge amounts of
space. RP Boo, DJ Rashad, DJ Spinn, Traxman, the Teklife crew. Footwork is dance music with
a literal dance attached: battle circles, two dancers, feet moving faster than seems
plausible. Rashad's *Double Cup* (2013) is the record that took it international. He died in
2014.

**The 90s house diaspora.** Cajmere / Green Velvet (Curtis Jones, who has a chemical
engineering degree from Berkeley, which you may enjoy), Cajual and Relief Records, DJ
Sneak, Derrick Carter, Mark Farina, Gene Farris, Paul Johnson. This is the generation that
made Chicago house a global club standard rather than a historical artefact.

## Why the local connection is worth something

Two practical reasons, beyond civic pride.

First, the source material is unusually accessible. You can go to Gramaphone, you can go to
the Chosen Few Picnic, you can stand outside 206 S. Jefferson. Most people learning a genre
are learning it from a distance.

Second, and more usefully: the Chicago tradition is technically forgiving. These records
were made on cheap gear by people who were learning as they went. The production is often
crude by modern standards, the mixes are strange, and none of that matters because the
groove and the feeling carry it. If you were trying to learn by imitating modern
festival-tier production you would be competing against people with twenty years of mixing
experience and mastering engineers. Imitating a 1986 Trax record is a fair fight.
